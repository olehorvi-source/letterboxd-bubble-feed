2026-09-14 The Surgeon (2026) davidehrlich secondary 2.5 no It’s been 18 years since “Taken” came out, and bad guys still haven’t learned not to fuck with — older, seemingly harmless — people who have a very special set of skills. Rarely have those skills been more special, or at least more specialized, than they are in Roshan Sethi’s “The Surgeon,” a sedate but gruesome action thriller in which sixtysomething Michelle Yeoh violates the Hippocratic Oath with extreme prejudice after some gangsters kidnap her to operate on their boss (Martin Freeman, selling the crime lord schtick better than you might expect from Bilbo Baggins). The movie is a few cuts short of a lasting suture, but it’s mighty fun to watch Yeoh prove her character’s point that “Surgery is precise violence.” We never learn a single detail about why Eliza Lim decided to devote her life to medicine, but the film’s opening flashback — in which a younger version of Yeoh’s role sits apart from her colleagues and brutally eviscerates a blood orange so that she can sew it up again — suggests that she wouldn’t know what to do with a social life if she had one. Cut to: Four decades later, and Eliza is being forced into retirement because the hospital can’t afford the liability. They say that after 60, the wrists start to shake. (Karan Soni, star of Sethi’s “A Nice Indian Boy,” has an amusing voice cameo as the State Farm agent responsible for insuring each of Eliza’s hands). But our heroine, who’s clearly still possessed by the superhuman poise that Yeoh has weaponized across her brilliant career, is unconvinced. After doing everything in her power to save people’s lives for so long, she can’t stomach the thought of just waiting for herself to die. Lucky for her, she may not have to wait as long as she thinks. She’s only been off the job for a few days before some goons snatch her out of her kitchen one morning and take her to a derelict hospital where their boss is already prepped for surgery in the operating theater. 2026-09-14 Love of Your Life (2026) davidehrlich secondary 3.5 no Rachel Morrison’s “Love of Your Life” is one of my favorite things, all too rare in today’s Hollywood: A gorgeously shot romantic drama about impossibly beautiful people that still manages to feel real enough where it matters. It’s the kind of smart, heartfelt, mid-weight entertainment that rewards you for surrendering to its logic and savoring how its light cuts through the darkness for 100 minutes — so naturally, since we live in hell, it’s only playing in theaters for a single week before it shuffles off to its forever home on Prime Video. I suppose we should be grateful that Morrison got to make it at all, as this is nothing if not a film that implores us to cherish what we still have left rather than rage at what’s been taken from us. It starts in a decidedly unromantic place: the emergency room of a Boston hospital, where joyless and withdrawn nurse Maya (Margaret Qualley) heals people as a way of attending to the unspecified family situation that scarred her as a child. Blasting Sharon Van Etten music on the subway and grinding out 18-hour shifts in her scrubs seems to be getting the job done, but happiness is a lot harder to deny when someone becomes determined to share it with you. In this case, that “someone” is Charlie (Gabriel Basso, far too charming for someone whose breakthrough role was playing J.D. Vance), a Mensa-smart goofball who smiles like a fool, dresses like a jock, and radiates an inner glow that shines a hole right through the lonerism that Maya has always maintained for her own protection. He was adopted at birth, and his worldview has been premised on accepting the love that he’s lucky enough to receive. Maya, by contrast, has a real “love isn’t worth the pain of losing it” vibe that has led her to stay as single and untethered as possible. At least until now. ~this review continues on IndieWire~
