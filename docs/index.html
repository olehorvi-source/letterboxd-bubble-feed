2026-09-16 Resident Evil (2026) davidehrlich secondary 3.5 no It’s hard to tell — and even harder to care — whether a small but attention-starved contingent of “Resident Evil” loyalists actually made a preemptive stink about Zach Cregger’s radical new take on the video game franchise, or if algorithmic rage cycles have become so predictable and endemic to the discourse that everyone just assumed that they had. Whatever the case, this much is clear: Cregger’s singular, madcap, arcade-style riff on the survival-horror series that started it all is a massive and rapidly mutating middle finger to the Comic-Con-brained idea that video game adaptations should just give “the fans” what they think they want. Cregger’s movie arrives in the wake of 2021’s “Welcome to Raccoon City,” a miserable cosplay which attempted to course-correct the nü-metal nuttiness of Paul W.S. Anderson’s movies by slavishly retelling the story of the first two “Resident Evil” titles. The “Weapons” director’s DLC-like approach to the zombie saga pivots back in the opposite direction with a giddy smile on its face. You’ll find no characters from those PlayStation classics (ditto their more recent sequels), nor any narrative connection to the series’ mythology beyond a shared location and a brief reference to the evil Umbrella Corporation. Easter eggs come by the dozen, but the movie’s fast, freaky undead more resemble — and behave like — the “Last of Us” infected than the slow-moving groaners from the early games. And yet, for all its creative liberties, this is the first “Resident Evil” movie that actually — and rather viscerally — evokes what it feels like to play “Resident Evil.” The white-knuckled helplessness of entering a new room and flinching at the way it’s framed to emphasize all the places a monster could jump out. The addictively queasy tug-of-war between wanting to explore the next section and wanting to just say “nope” and turn the whole thing off. The headache of item management, the thrill of blowing a villager’s head off with your last shotgun round, only to see tentacles sprout out of its neck hole as it grows more powerful. Cregger’s side-quest-sized story about a hapless courier has been genetically engineered in an Umbrella Corp. lab as the perfect vehicle for all these sense memories, to the point that his “Resident Evil” better reflects the essence of its source material than any of the previous movies. That’s despite how it eschews the franchise’s melodramatic tone in favor of postmodern glee, and ditches its deep(ly ridiculous) mythology in favor of… well, pretty much nothing aside from some errant anxieties about bringing a kid into a world that’s evolving out of our control. That’s what you get when you hire the guy who made “Weapons,” I suppose. A lean, gripping, and relentless goof absent any trace of the cart-before-the-horse ambitions that have crushed the life out of blockbuster cinema in the age of I.P., Cregger’s “Resident Evil” is so cut to the bone that it starts mid-sentence and drops its closing title card at what feels like might be the start of its third act. In other words, some poor schmuck is about to have the worst night of his life, and he’s only gonna need about 85 minutes to do it. ~this review continues on IndieWire~

2026-09-16 Possible Love (2026) thejoshl primary 4.0 no You might be surprised to learn that Lee Chang-dong made another lyrical, thorny, novelistic portrait of South Korea's yearning, futureless working-class as told via an ambiguous and bleak romance-drama; and even brought back his two leads from Peppermint Candy and Secret Sunshine to do it! Loosely informed by a real-life labor strike and the Dekalog mandate that "thou shalt not covet", his latest intimate-epic densely and patiently traces out the complicated "friendship" between two couples: a stoic laid-off factory worker who was violently assaulted during strike + his wife who has born painful witness to his change in dignity and behavior since, and a documentary filmmaker interested in making a film about them (and the subject of the "vanishing laborer") + her generationally wealthy husband. Similarly to Burning , Lee is both fascinated by the moving interpersonal dynamics these people offer each other (one opening up about the suffering they've experienced and satisfying their curiosity, the other allowing those same people to briefly dream and fantasize and be within the proximity of material comforts "like a place from a novel [they] haven't read"), while also accumulating striking class detail and resentment that complicates and corrupts any chance of a genuine relationship. Empathetically exploring the sad reality that so many people's sense of dignity and identity is directly tied to their job and what they can provide their family with (and the unjust humiliation, rage and emptiness of that), and the fundamental inability for people for whom money has never been a concern to connect with those people. Lee constantly weaponizing the beautifully controlled long-take docudrama photography and non-linear flashback editing, and using both to gracefully flesh out how this contradiction invisibly seeps into casual conversations and transactions, and eventually boils into a nakedly primal scream. Layering in all kinds of astonishingly small details that accumulate power as the pressure builds: the way they refer to those kept their jobs in the corporate restructuring as "the living" vs. those who lost them as "the dead"; the way all the drinking is connected back to inciting violence, the physicality in the two different scenes of the kid doing taekwondo, the police whistle (as it both relates to the traumatic police violence sequence + the property worker), the car washing memory, the way the camera keeps intruding the frame or disrupting the flow of conversation, the full moon!! Lee has such a gift for getting you attuned to the subjectivity and of seemingly inconsequential details of these character lives and so thoroughly realizing them that a minor moment as simple as a mom watching her kid spray a tree with a hose is loaded with an entire generational history of labor vs. capital. What a filmmaker.
