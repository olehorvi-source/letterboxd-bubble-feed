2026-11-20 The Weight (2026) thejoshl primary 3.0 no Really solid directorial debut. Decidedly old-school in setting and style, combining elements of Depression-era chain gang prison dramas, westerns, survival-thrillers, men-on-a-mission movies, etc. The procedural situation itself of desperate laborers mucking it through the wilderness on a potential suicide mission with gold on their backs brings to mind The Wages of Fear and Treasure of Sierra Madre for sure, but its got a few tricks of its own in terms of rendering the tense, gruff arduousness of this work and the power collaboration/solidarity with your fellow man. Highlights: the rickety rope bridge brick-throwing, the log jam river near-drowning, and the Southern Comfort boat stealing that turns into a stormy middle-of-the-night smothering. Having such a hopeful ending kinda surprised me and felt a little sudden/clean after milking such bleak suspense for so long, but even if I wished it hit me a bit a harder down the stretch there was still plenty to like here: some moody and effective editing choices, a great ambient score (featuring elements of acoustic, electronic and rock sounds), and Hawke is as per usual terrific.

2026-11-19 The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth (2026) davidehrlich secondary 3.0 no The scratchy “vintage” Netflix logo says everything so well that the rest of the movie almost feels like it’s belaboring the point: “The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth” is a story about a town where half the people are dreaming up the future, the other half are desperate to mythologize the past, and all of them are stuck together in the ugly compromise we call the right fucking now. Then again, “story” might be too strong a word for the unbothered zen noir that Quentin Tarantino has spun off from his 1969-set hit “Once Upon a Time in Hollywood,” which memorably ended with former TV Western star Rick Dalton and his longtime stuntman Cliff Booth saving Manson Family victim Sharon Tate from the cruel fate that befell her in real life. Los Angeles was built on the promise that history could be rewritten, and that anyone who made their way onto a studio lot could print the legend, but the present is another beast altogether. It’s feral. Unforgiving. It has to be survived before it can be shaped into a story worth telling, even if that isn’t much of a story at all. After decades of trafficking in ultra-fetishistic nostalgia (and continued years of refusing to close the book on his own legend), Tarantino has scripted a conditional surrender to the forward march of time — a film whose modest and spectacularly unbothered protagonist invites his egomaniacal and constantly agitated creator to make a certain peace with the inevitable fact of being left behind. And though it was initially Tarantino’s plan to direct that film himself, handing the reins of this breezy showbiz throwback to modern Hollywood’s most unsentimental technician forces “The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth” into a muted but fascinating tug-of-war between the past tense and the future perfect (even if that scratchy “vintage” Netflix logo articulates the same tension with a greater clarity of purpose than any other part of this perverse collaboration). Pleasant enough for a movie so dark, and strangely relaxed for a movie so deeply self-conflicted, “The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth” is ultimately a story — there’s that word again — about someone changing with the times without letting the times change them in return. It takes place over the span of a few days in the summer of 1977 (even though it builds to the October premiere of “Looking for Mr. Goodbar,” which I suppose might have happened a few months earlier in this parallel universe), when “Star Wars” is the biggest force on Earth and the tough guys of yore are being phased out by the very different kind of daring that New Hollywood brought to the table. Once upon a time, Cliff Booth did his business supporting Rick Dalton on the big screen — reborn here as the lead of a David Fincher film, he’s reintroduced working the projector at the New Beverly Cinema like the hyper-chill uncle Tyler Durden never had (digital cigarette burns and all). If “The Further Mis-Adventures” doesn’t belabor the reel change from one generation of movies to another, that’s because Cliff doesn’t either. He’s just happy to be there — still hot, cool, and at total peace with himself despite an increasingly violent reputation (a state of mind Brad Pitt might relate to). The world around him is a lot uglier than it was eight years ago, as the vibrant celluloid memory palace of Tarantino’s film has faded into the computational muddiness that Fincher has come to mistake for the truth, but that “don’t mean shit” to Cliff. ~this review continues on IndieWire~
