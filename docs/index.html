2026-09-11 Club Kid (2026) thejoshl primary 4.0 no "Is this a good cry or a bad cry?" "I don't know." Really beautiful and immersive depiction of the queer NYC nightlife scene inside a Sundance dramedy about a party/drug-addict who realizes he should probably go back to school and get a job once adulthood is suddenly thrust upon him in the form of a cute kid with good taste in music. Part of me felt this was a little too surprisingly easy and frictionless as an actual father-son drama for a good part of its runtime, though honestly that was mostly made up for by how comically "untraditional" the details of said relationship and environment were. (Lost it at the "Fucking bitch!! You're gonna let him talk like that?!" bit.) Before realizing that that was the point being made here, and it planned on dropping the hammer the entire time. So congrats to everyone involved, I teared up exactly on command, and while doing so realized there's definitely something a lot sneakier and more interesting about how Firstman is using the conventions of a formulaic "universal" story than I at first thought. Especially with how those beats dynamically interact with the broader community portraiture, which is both completely clear-eyed about all the sex and drugs, but also loving and non-judgmental about it, and keenly aware of its existence as an alternative to the "traditional" family structures queer people are often denied or abused within/running away from. Really moving stuff.

2026-09-11 The Unknown (2026) thejoshl primary 4.0 no Skipped the line for Fjord to see the movie that sounded more my shit—a Vincent Gallo-esque loner-drifter-photographer hooks up at a party with Lea Seydoux and then suddenly realizes he's in a French arthouse body-swap thriller with some It Follows rules—and wasn't disappointed. Seydoux, as expected, is phenomenal. Getting to do her own Under the Skin and Enemy as she eerily, uncomfortably wanders Paris both getting acclimated to her new body and procedurally investigating who (or what) is in her old one. There's enough formal rigor and creepy mood to earn the comparisons you might hear to Lynch and De Palma's obsession with Hitchcock's doppelganger images/voyeur-thriller lensing, but Antonioni is definitely the North Star. Not just in terms of the Blow-Up photography and mystery aspect but the confident sense of ambiguity and confusion this is comfortable leaving you to sift through. Whether that's the complicated nature of identity and gender (every sex scene in this is unsettling and sad) or becoming a disembodied witness to yourself and the void you would leave your loved reckoning with if you disappeared. There's even some playful movie-within-a-movie and dream artifice, and some insane music choices including The Weeknd Bob Dylan... A Less Complete Unknown ? Is this anything? Anyway, surprised the French didn't get more out of this one at Cannes.
2026-09-11 All of a Sudden (2026) thejoshl primary 3.0 no "Are healthy people truly alive?" If you liked all the therapy through smoking, talking and experimental theatre in Drive My Car boy do I have good news for you! Unfortunately, this one didn't quite hit me as hard, even if this is undeniably getting at a lot of interesting threads regarding the sensitivity and dignity of healthcare, work/life balance (mental and labor exhaustion), Marxist theory, art as tool for radical expression and healing, etc... The performances are excellent, both from the two leads apparently reading dialogue taken directly from real letters between a terminally ill philosopher Makiko Mayano/medical anthropologist Maho Isono, and the non-actor patients they're surrounded with as they discuss the humanity of the (historically abused) institutionalized, and how to ensure caregiving (like art) remains an act of gentle, soulful connection even when the business side of it tries to reduce it to cold numbers on a page. It's a really beautiful idea expressed in some unique ways through Hamaguchi's feel for simple and meditative craftmanship (the theatre performance stuff is once again astonishing), and also some less unique ones that at times got a little too bright, didactic and sentimental for me personally... like the most radical and artful dramaturgical realization of scenes you might see in a 90s dramedy where Robin Williams teaches some kids a lesson or gives a therapy session. There's also way more of the epic runtime dedicated to most of the ensemble giving or receiving foot rubs than I could've possibly expected. That said, there are very few filmmakers working today as in tune with the art of conversation as Ryusuke Hamaguchi is, and the middle section here that plays like his Before Sunset is great.

2026-09-10 Everytime (2026) davidehrlich secondary 4.5 yes I don’t believe in an afterlife, but that’s OK because the people I love most are never going to die. Not to me. At least, not in the way they used to before our species began to migrate online, to store our memories in the cloud, and to digitally encode the raw data of our existence onto everything we touch. My father was born in 1939, the same year that Iowa State College scientists John Vincent Atanasoff and Clifford Berry built the prototype for the first binary-based computer; in the months after he died 75 years later, I often felt the reflex to reach down and call him from the iPhone that fit inside my pocket. To ask if he’d seen the Rangers game, or to tell him that I’d gotten engaged. I knew that he was gone, of course, but the piece of consumer technology I’d used to connect with him when he was alive had created a separate neural pathway between my brain and his being — a crude one-way network that didn’t begin to break down until much later in my grieving process. A decade later, those pathways have grown exponentially more complicated and convincing. The emergence of AI has led to a variety of ghoulish attempts at deathlessness and resurrection (from bots that feed on your grandma’s Facebook data to the ChatGPT Theodore Roosevelt who Trump was “honored to be with today” at the former’s Presidential Library), but such products and publicity stunts miss the fact that everyone is already being (temporarily) immortalized on a server farm somewhere, the traces of our existence shed into the digital ether like a Pollock-like splotch of invisible ink. Charlie Kirk’s face is Twitter’s most ubiquitous meme. My long-deceased college friend you’ve never heard of sends me hacked emails full of links to “old pictures they found of us together.” The cancer-stricken hedgehog I fostered for a few months in 2016 still cycles onto my home screen at random sometimes, and he’s now been buried behind a house in suburban New Jersey for longer than he was ever alive. People have always looked for errant glimmers of the dead in the world around them, but now the past runs parallel to — imprints upon, and circles around — the present to a degree that can severely confuse the relationship between the two. Sandra Wollner doesn’t believe in an afterlife either, but she’s certainly attuned to the mysteries of this one. Picking up where the equally spectral likes of “Personal Shopper” and “Aftersun” left up, the Austrian writer/director’s mystical and magnificently beguiling “Everytime” takes a rather ambivalent stance towards what happens when people die these days, or at least — per its curious title — towards how we mourn them from inside the flat circle of a temporal overlap where yesterday and tomorrow are folded into the pockets of an ever-expanding now. There are no clear answers to be found in this astonishing third feature, which shares a number of thematic similarities with Wollner’s “The Trouble with Being Born,” but retains one crucial difference (that film, about a man who has an implied sexual relationship with a child-sized android coded with the memories of his missing 10-year-old daughter, unsurprisingly had trouble securing American distribution). Indeed, the grounded portraiture of the movie’s first 70 minutes eventually gives way to a final act so cosmic and surreal that it becomes all but impossible to pinpoint the precise location of Wollner’s characters in space-time; to say for certain whether “Everytime” has glitched into another dimension, or if it has invited another dimension to glitch into our own. Initially timeless in its nuanced and deeply recognizable depiction of loss, and then even more so as it gets devoured by the meta-modern flourishes that have fringed its story from the start, this is perhaps the most forward-thinking film I’ve ever seen about the changing face of grief. ~this review continues on IndieWire~
