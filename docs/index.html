2026-10-31 The Devils (1971) thejoshl primary 5.0 yes “I have been a man. I have loved women. I have enjoyed power." “Do you love the Church?” “Not today.” Even watching a crummy DVD rip 8 years ago when I first started podcasting about movies like this, it was already clear that Ken Russell’s violently hysterical, feverishly cinematic realization of Aldous Huxley’s non-fiction accounting + John Whiting’s west end theatre adaptation of French Catholic priest Urbain Grandier’s burning at the stake for the crime of rebelling against religious celibacy and allegedly seducing a convent of Ursuline nuns (which was subsequently deemed an act of satanic possession by corrupt church and state figures politically motivated to scapegoat and destroy him) was an unsung masterpiece unfairly buried and lost to time due to a conservative censorship campaign and outdated accusations of sacrilegious pornography. And if you would’ve told me then that I would eventually see this in its fully restored, uncut X-rated glory on a film print in my lifetime I wouldn’t have believed you yet here we are… What a surreal yet appropriate way to spend Devil’s Night. Russell certainly courted many of the controversies he suffered by stylishly leaning into the grotesque obscenity of his rendering of 17th century France, contrasting the gorgeously decadent historical production design with the rotten reality of this plague-ridden environment of mass graves, mad doctors and oppressive religious hypocrisy. A contrast that also finds a home in the psychological, political and performance duel between Oliver Reed’s handsome, charismatic and womanizing Father Grandier (“a man worth going to hell for”) whose desire for the church to recognize sexual desire as natural is recognized as both technically progressive yet also sinfully self-serving, and Vanessa Redgrave’s deeply lonely, repressed and resentful hunchbacked nun Sister Jeanne, whose psychosexual attraction to Grandier spirals into gruesome fetishization of Catholic suffering/torture/martyrdom and eventually, inadvertently becomes a cynically manipulated weapon for the consolidation of state power. It isn’t particularly original or subtle in its agenda to deconstruct the machinations of how fascists have historically demonized popular figures who threaten their authority, but the delirious, frenzied and mesmerizingly hellish manner it goes about expressing it is the source of its uniquely nauseating power. It’s the surreal dreaminess of how it depicts Sister Jeanne’s psychosexual obsession with Grandier (envision him in place of a crucified Jesus, erotically licking his wounds), the irreverent debauched mania of its infamous “Rape of Christ” orgy sequence, the overwhelming lavish filthiness and noisy circus made out of this obvious scheme to manipulate the masses, and the unflinching commitment to seeing the vivid brutality of Grandier’s humiliating, leg-shattering, fiery Passion of Joan of Arc punishment through. By the time Sister Jeanne is upsettingly, pathetically masturbating with the charred femur of the man she so intensely lusted for, the only response at that point is uncomfortable, exhausted laughter at the sheer sweeping theatricality and morbidly horrific degradation on display. Whether that gaudy, maximalist approach to historical and spiritual subject matter usually handled with elegance and austerity is productive or exploitative was part of a lot of critic’s frustrations with it back in 1971, but decades later there’s something to be said about passionate moral outrage deliberately, ostentatiously conveyed in such an arguably immoral, outrageous fashion. “Call me vain and proud, the greatest sinner ever to walk God's earth. But Satan's boy I could never be. I haven't the humility.” [ 35mm ] Full discussion on ep 30 of my podcast SLEAZOIDS .
