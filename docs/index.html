2026-11-04 Rope (1948) thejoshl primary 4.0 yes “Murder can be an art too… The power to kill can be just as satisfying as the power to create.” It makes a lot of sense that an adaptation of a stage play inspired by a real-life gay couple who committed murder as an intellectual exercise would itself be treated as a formal exercise by the master of the visual language of murder stories. The elegant, gliding long-take oner illusion (assembled from a dozen or so shots) is occasionally stretched beyond the point of claustrophobic pressure cooker effectiveness, but it’s a lot less ostentatiously gimmicky than its reputation. Hitchcock for the most part keeps his fluid camera movement economical and functional, drifting in and out of anxious character perspective in a way that mostly sustains the morbid, real-time tension of Brandon and Phillip’s comically perverse dinner party where a bunch of bitchy melodrama is infected by the dramatic irony of us knowing where the strangled corpse in the room is right from the opening shot. It’s a cool subversion of the murder mystery genre to make it not really a mystery at all and instead focus on the comedy of manners between this subtextually (but also hilariously obviously) gay couple and the guests they're hosting, as well as the (im)moral Nietzschean philosophizing/sociopathic horniness that inspired their inciting crime. How often that wooden chest hangs out on the periphery of the frame as guilt continues to mount, or the camera suddenly adopts the POV focus of incriminating objects as they become fearful of their discovery is where this technique is working at its finest. I particularly like the bit in where Rupert theorizes the process of how to commit a murder in such a space out loud to the killers, and the camera adopts his sightlines as he goes step-by-step through it; arriving at the hiding of the body part and clearly looking towards chest while deceptively denying the conclusion we just saw him come to and saying he would actually take the corpse out the door. Despite his own retrospective objections to the casting, Jimmy Stewart is very good at doing this suspicious, perceptive detective routine, observing the bizarre details and seams of this elaborately “perfect” murder plan and piecing it together, but I do find his didactic moral outrage arc over these wealthy delusional murderers quite silly... He goes from being like “murder is so sick, it’s awesome as hell, a work of art in many ways, we should start doing The Purge” to [sees dead body for the first time] “wait wtf this sucks ass I’m sorry I’m trying to delete it” so quickly I've never really fully bought it. Anyway, sick movie about doing crimes with your annoying buddy. And that movable multi-room set is unbelievable; the nighttime skyline painting with glowing neon signs leaking through the windows during the finale is beautiful. 2026-11-03 Klara and the Sun (2026) davidehrlich secondary no I’ve seen enough: Taika Waititi is no longer riffing on a level that is frickin’ sublime. A quick update for those of you who don’t get your news from DiscussingFilm and/or obsessively listen to “Who? Weekly” in order to keep tabs on Rita Ora: On Saturday afternoon, writer-director Taika Waititi opened Instagram and posted — to his grid! — a less than equanimous reaction to the reviews of his new motion picture “Klara and the Sun,” which was in the process of making less money per screen during its second weekend than the average movie theater employee. It wasn’t the most embarrassing self-own of the pre-midterms weekend (that honor belongs to Michigan congresswoman Hayley Stevens, who committed political suicide on live TV with Anderson Cooper), but I’d argue that Waititi’s faux pas has more to say about how the reality distortion field of today’s media landscape has made it all too easy for highly visible people to misperceive the world around them in a crisis. “I haven’t read a review of anything aimed at me or my peers in almost 15 years,” the director wrote under an image celebrating the 53-point mismatch between the film’s audience and critics scores on Rotten Tomatoes. “But I do listen to the people, so this Halloween, let’s spare a thought for those sad lost souls who couldn’t achieve their dreams of becoming artists, and thus became critics. The same people who also hated ‘Jojo Rabbit 😂 🤷🏽‍♂️.’ Irrelevant. Out of touch. Obsolete. In the bin.🗑️😢🫶🏽.” When I saw that his post had been modified, I assumed that Waititi had deleted the part where he wrote “and another thing: I’m not mad. Please don’t put in the newspaper that I got mad.” In truth, he had simply tweaked the bit where it said that “sad lost souls” like me had settled as critics. Good note! Much as fallen angels plummet from the heavens before landing in the depths of Hell to anguish for all eternity, critics don’t settle for a life of press screenings so much as we pathetically free fall into it, plummeting from the sacred highs of making “Thor: Love & Thunder” to the wretched lows of watching it. It’s that natural eye for editing that explains why “Next Goal Wins” felt like it had been cut with a soccer cleat. Of course, I don’t take any of this too personally. (Don’t you dare fucking put in the newspaper that I got mad.) For one thing, I was actually quite tolerant of “Thor: Love & Thunder,” if only because I believe that casting Christian Bale as a guy named “Gorr the God Butcher” in and of itself makes that movie better than most of what Marvel was pumping out at the time. For another thing, I haven’t even seen “Klara and the Sun” — not because I was busy when it screened here, or because I live in one of the many countries where its release has been canceled on account of the movie’s supposed awfulness and verifiable lack of public interest, but rather because I adore the Kazuo Ishiguro novel on which it’s based, and I was convinced that hiring the director of “Jojo Rabbit” to adapt such a poignant tale of loss and brokenness would be like hiring Peter Farrelly to remake “The Remains of the Day.” Except worse, because I’ve always felt like the Merchant Ivory version of that book could have used a few gratuitous jokes about Anthony Hopkins’ swollen balls. The truth is that the perceived quality of Waititi’s film is irrelevant to what I found interesting about his Instagram post, in much the same way as its quality is seemingly irrelevant to everything else as well. And in much the same way as the perceived quality of Waititi’s other films is irrelevant to the hissy fit he decided to throw about this one. Are “the same people who also hated ‘Jojo Rabbit’” in the room with us? Because that Oscar-winning movie is sitting at 81 percent on Rotten Tomatoes (Certified Fresh™!). OK sure, I hated it, but, as we’ve previously established, I am quite literally not “the same people” who hated “Klara and the Sun.” On the other hand, I would very much consider myself to be among the large cohort of critics who adored Waititi’s earlier features like “Hunt for the Wilderpeople,” “What We Do in the Shadows,” and “Eagle vs. Shark.” I suppose I could look up the aggregator metrics for those movies as well, but there isn’t any need — that most of the people reading this article have even heard of such modest Kiwi treasures is testament enough to the groundswell of critical support they received around the world. Perhaps, if he’d read a review in the last 15 years, Waititi might have been able to put that together for himself. ~this article continues on IndieWire~
