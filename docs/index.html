In the present day, an alcoholic gym teacher named Sergio (Michele Riondino) — nursing some recent grief of his own — is relocated to Remis, only to find that the surviving townspeople have never been happier, despite the shared tragedy that would seem to overshadow their lives. It’s uncanny. It’s disturbing. It’s what Sergio wants for himself more than anything in the world. And he’s told that all he has to do to make the pain go away is to hug “The Holy Boy” mentioned in the English title of Paolo Strippoli’s third feature, a slick and effectively spine-tickling horror movie about the harm sown into people convinced they can avoid their own hurt. (It’s also one hell of a calling card for the young Italian director, whose recent festival hit “The Spiral” will receive an Oscar-qualifying release later this year, and an English-language remake sometime after that). For a time, Sergio becomes one of those people. When he first arrives in Remis, sobbing in his car as it chugs up the curvy mountain roads, it feels like a cruel joke that the surly ex-Judo champion has been relocated to a place known as “The Valley of Smiles.” He’s a belligerent asshole to the kids in his P.E. class, acts violently toward the beautiful waitress (Romana Maggiora Vergano as Michela) at the local bar, and generally brings a “Bad Santa” energy to a town with more of a “Pleasantville” vibe. But from the start, it’s clear Remis hides a secret darkness — a darkness so deep that none of the residents can see clearly that they live in a horror film. It isn’t normal for a weasel-eyed man to stand in the bushes and glare at the village high school all day. It isn’t normal for everyone to meet in a barn on the outskirts of town in the middle of the night and participate in a vaguely Catholic ritual that sees them all line up for a chance to lay their hands on a sullen 15-year-old kid (Giulio Feltri as Matteo). It isn’t normal for them to feel like they might go insane, or worse, if they go too long without his touch. Give people a panacea for their pain, and they’ll do anything to keep it, logic be damned. Of course, logic is hardly the only thing that’s damned in “The Holy Boy,” whose concussive soundtrack, broadly demonic symbology (e.g., dropped coins transforming into bloody egg yolks), and genre-inflected visual motifs (lots of upside-down camerawork and clever uses of soft focus) help consecrate this high-altitude “Twilight Zone” story’s rather literal approach to elevated horror. But Strippoli’s script, co-written by Jacopo Del Giudice and Milo Tissone, isn’t content to settle for mere social allegory, even if the movie tacks harder in that direction with every new detail we learn about Matteo’s effect on the people who touch him. 2026-11-25 The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth (2026) brofromanother primary yes This review may contain spoilers. Review for Ringer: www.theringer.com/2026/11/25/movies/the-further-misadventures-of-cliff-booth-review-brad-pitt-david-fincher-quentin-tarantino The last time Quentin Tarantino featured a film critic as a character in one of his scripts, the poor guy turned out to be slightly dumber than he looked. Michael Fassbender’s Operation Kino mastermind Lt. Archie Hickox may have known plenty about G.W. Pabst, Leni Riefenstahl, and The White Hell of Pitz Palu but after making an “unorthodox three finger gesture” in a German tavern, he blows his cover and goes out speaking the King’s beneath that stiff upper lip.. There’s no similarly violent fate befalling Karen Crosby, the former freelance movie reviewer played by Elizabeth Debicki inThe Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth. She is, though, stranded behind enemy lines. Plunked down at a Hollywood country club in June of 1977 with her TV-star boss Charlie Peters (Scott Caan), his ex-starlet wife Toni (Carla Gugino) and seasoned stuntman Cliff Booth (Brad Pitt)—the latter of whom has been doing his best to seduce the statuesque Australian away from her unseen fiancee—Karen raises her seatmates’ collective hackles by claiming that the movies they all make, and which she used to dutifully sit through to craft capsule-length pans for a tiny readership, aren’t art but trash. Exhibit A: Star Wars, which she dismisses flippantly as a “technically superior version of Flash Gordon.” If Karen’s anti-blockbuster spiel sounds suspiciously familiar, that’s because QT has written the character expressly as a “Paulette:” a disciple of the legendary New Yorker film critic Pauline Kael. Over the course of this long, languorous lunch—an extension of a mixed doubles game organized by bigshot-VIP Charlie, who’s courting Cliff for semi-professional reasons of his own—we learn that Kael took a shine to Karen and her writing, and even made some calls on her behalf (shades of Paul Schrader, who’s told similar stories over the years). Karen’s own career shift from ink-stained wretch to artistically stifled agent moping on the fringes of A-list premieres can’t help but recall Kael’s real-life sojourn in the Paramount C-suite, where she was lured in diabolical bad faith by Warren Beatty in a power move designed to cut a major tastemaker off at the knees. It seems that Kael’s brilliance in diagnosing the problems with American cinema didn’t translate into a gift for helping to make it: In an article published in Newsweek about her exile from criticism in Hollywood, one studio insider noted that the studio’s controversial new hire “did a masterful job of alienating everybody within six weeks.” “It's like getting a box of Cracker Jack which is all prizes,” Kael wrote of Star Wars. Karen echoes Kael’s disdainful rhetoric—although certainly not her authentic and enduring cinephilia—while offering up a junk food metaphor of her own: “They’re hamburgers, shovelled to the undiscerning masses,” she says of the current crop of new releases. “Critics are wasting their lives dissecting the minor differences between a Big Mac and a Whopper.” Karen’s palette is refined to the point of snobbery: for her, there’s not much difference between The Godfather and Toni’s cheesy late ’60s kiddie-movie breakthrough Salty the Seal. She’d rather read Kerouac or Camus. Fair enough, although given her fondness for all things French and pretentious, one wonders if she saw many foreign-language films on her freelance beat. Would she agree with her mentor that Alain Resnais was a brainy fraud throwing the cinematic equivalent of “come dressed as the sick soul of Europe parties”? Would she have called Last Year at Marienbad a Royale With Cheese? There’s surely plenty to beef with in Tarantino’s only official book of film criticism, Cinema Speculation, and plenty to chew on as well. Ditto his sprawling, exhausting novelization of Once Upon a Time … in Hollywood, which reveals, amongst many, many other altered plot points, that war-hero-slash-possible-wife-killer-slash-hippie-hater-slash-pit-bull-afficiando Cliff Booth is also—wait for it—a hardcore cinephile who manfully mainlines Bergman (Per Cliff: boring), Fellini (indulgent), and Antonioni (a fraud) in order to shake off the complacency of late ’60s American studio dreck. (He does hate Resnais—another Paulette in training.) Because Tarantino, the former Manhattan beach tape-slinger, loves the sound of his own voice, Cliff’s thumbs-up-or-down judgments are given plenty of space. But because QT, the storyteller, is usually not nearly as dumb as he looks (or sounds), these observations are load-bearing to his chosen theme of industrial paradigm shift. On the page and on the screen, One Upon a Time … in Hollywood is about the changing of the artistic guard and the bruised emotions that attend obsolescence. Even before the sublime Rolling Stones montage that provides the movie version with its most haunting and beautiful moment, we know that Cliff, for all his stubborn, middle-aged vitality, is out of time. With this in mind, The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth—directed, I should note by now, by David Fincher and not Tarantino, who’s apparently waiting to write a more appropriate script for his self-prophesized 10th-and-final-feature—can’t help but feel superfluous. That’s not a put-down, by the way. If it seems like I’ve been talking around the actual storyline of Fincher’s film, it’s because so much of its pleasures reside on the periphery precisely where Tarantino has put them, and where Fincher—in what is probably the loosest and most relaxed piece of film direction on his resume since the most picaresque passages of The Curious Case of Benjamin Button—keeps them. It’s worth noting that Kael’s shadow, like her meditations on the apparent flaws of auteur theory and the intrinsic contradictions between art and commerce at the heart of all American moviemaking—fell over the director’s 2021 feature Mank, about the battle over creative credit for Citizen Kane. The complementary virtuosities of Tarantino’s screenwriting (at once digressive and propulsive, with jagged, fragmented edges that lock into place) and Fincher’s image-making (as swift and witty as ever, with plenty of sight gags derived from advertising signage and television commercials) make a persuasive case for a brand-name collaboration. If the movie has a refrain, it’s “It don’t mean shit to me”: said once by a character in a movie-within-the-movie; once in real life by the actor playing him; and once by a fan of said actor in imitation of his leading-man charisma.There’s plenty of implication in there: The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth is a throwaway with deceptively careful aim. To use Karen’s own flame-broiled metaphor, it’s a gourmet nothingburger in which two great—if toxic—tastes taste merely pretty good together. That people might be skeptical about this particular film’s nutritional value is—to quote its namesake—fair enough. Speaking with Josh Horowitz on Happy Sad Confused, Fincher protested—a bit too much, perhaps—about the perception he’s lost his fastball during his exclusive deal with Netflix, which is also distributing Cliff Booth: “I don't get, like, I'm washed up and haven't had a good movie since Gone Girl,” he said. “And then you go, ‘Well, but this masterpiece that you just turned your back on and why?’ Well, that was another four years of my life, and I don’t understand why that’s so hard to absorb.” The “masterpiece” in question is Mindhunter, several episodes of which do rank with Fincher’s best work; even if Mank and The Killer do not, they’re still fascinating, personal works that feel of a piece with his filmography—not least of all as oblique self-portraits. The Killer is especially resonant in this regard: The shot of Fassbender in the coda, contemplating whether he’s escaped the corporate machine or entered a simulacrum of independence, vibrates with useful ambiguity. And then there’s controversy over Fincher’s alleged use of generative A.I. Leaving aside the fact that the claims made by several attendees of early screenings on social media have not been formally addressed by the director one way or another (the quotes from Fincher about “not being a Luddite” are from a Los Angeles Times interview printed before the online furor), it’s fair to say that The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth features sequences that feel suspicious, as if staged (or stranded) on the wrong side of the uncanny valley. Rather than speculating about whether or not the accusations are true—or if they’re disqualifying, for this movie or the many others that are either already using similar shortcuts or will be soon enough— I’d say that 1) Fincher’s track record makes him a likely suspect for such experimentation and 2) the film’s cinematography, by Mank Oscar winner Eric Messerschmidt, revels in the tensions between artificially analog textures. Given Tarantino’s prevailing celluloid fetishism, it must have been an act of Herculean restraint for him to watch Fincher shoot digitally, but the results here are more beguiling than the anachronistic format play of Mank: Where Once Upon a Time … in Hollywood was shot predominantly at magic-hour, its follow-up is more muted, suiting a story and protagonist defined by shades of grey. The romance between Cliff and Karen—and the chemistry between Pitt and Debicki—is the most absorbing element of The Further Mis-Adventures, not least of all because Tarantino does his best to structure the movie around them as a series of interruptions. Because the film is so carefully plotted, I’m hesitant to give too much away; suffice it to say that Cliff is pretty much where we left him at the end of Once Upon a Time…, except that now instead of living with his dog behind a drive-in movie theatre he’s working as a projectionist at the New Beverly Cinema (insert obligatory reference to QT’s biography and theater ownership here) and crashing with his father Gus (Peter Weller), an elderly pawn shop owner who seems cognizant of but unconcerned with his son’s unseemly extra-curricular activities. It’s not like Cliff is actually making his living threading blaxploitation pictures for appreciative late-night crowds; nor has he cashed in on the heroism he showed at the climax of the previous film. Instead, he’s content to give the credit for the Cielo Drive massacre to his buddy/doppelganger Rick Dalton—deployed wisely by Tarantino as a structuring absence, stashed away shooting an Outback-style Western series in Australia—and ply his dark arts in the shadows of Hollywood Babylon: i.e. retrieving a very famous (and apparently closeted) auteur’s Oscar after it goes missing as part of a skeezy honeypot scheme enacted by a young gay hustler, Joey (Corey Fogelmanis), and his cynical pimp (Karren Karagulian). Our anti-hero’s encounter with the hapless blackmailers takes place entirely at the New Beverly, which he—and Fincher and Tarantino—treat reverently as home turf. We’re cued to see that as long as Cliff is in a movie theater, he has the upper hand—the same way he did in the familiar confines of Spahn Ranch in Once Upon a Time… The brutal effortlessness with which Cliff establishes dominance is also a carryover from the earlier film, but the (relative) decency with which he resolves the situation— by leaving one adversary alive and by protecting the wounded dignity of his older, melancholy employer—speaks to Tarantino’s shifting depiction of the character, which is that he’s going slightly soft. This is a mixed blessing, conceptually speaking. The set-up of Cliff as a plausible sociopath in civilian clothing gave Once Upon a Time… its pressurized sense of danger and also a necessary dose of moral ambiguity (along with an Oscar for Pitt). Now, Cliff’s SoCal uber-mensch act has been watered down like the Daiquiris he pours at his other part time job: He’s kind to his co-workers, dotes on his Dad, and holds his tongue when Karen condescends to the art form he knows and loves—and not just because, as he so indelicately (but, in a fashion, romantically) puts it during one conversation, he’s been thinking of her consistently in private, intimate moments. For those viewers who take most of Tarantino’s dialogue as verbal masturbation, Cliff’s admission probably won’t seem charming. Still, Pitt and Debicki inhabit their scenes’ mix of adolescent juvenalia and recognizably grown-up desire with aplomb. “We’re all adults here,” says Cliff at one point to Toni, with whom he had a long-ago fling—unbeknownst to Charlie, who’d just get jealous, and very much beknownst to Karen, who’s visibly stewing as soon as she finds out—and darned if the interplay between these characters, and the treatment of personal and sexual relationships in general, doesn’t feel, well, grown-up. This is the closest Tarantino has come to true maturity in this department since Jackie Brown (which happens to be the QT film that The Further Mis-Adventures evokes more, in many ways, than Once Upon a Time… even as narrative and character elements from the latter can’t help but take pride of place). At one point, Cliff looks up at the television to see a commercial starring Margot Robbie’s Sharon Tate; it’s a wonderfully ambivalent little moment, balancing warmth against a kind of rueful resignation that Tate’s alternate-universe happily-ever-after includes an obligatory sideline shilling for cosmetic companies. There are other, more front and center means by which Tarantino resurrects—and revises—the basic storyline of the earlier film. Karen is pursuing Cliff for Charlie, whom she assumes is either trying to entice him to come out of retirement as a stuntman—despite the fact that Charlie both does his own stunts and is too short-statured and springy to be doubled by a limping six-footer—while Charlie is pursuing Cliff for his behind-the-scenes savvy. It seems that while shooting a movie in the Manilla, Charlie befriended a local crime lord, Verdiusco, and became imbricated with him and his trigger-happy, psychopathic adult sons Eddie (Matt Groove) and Fernando (Jay Blanco). In a moment of alcohol-and-fellatio fuelled weakness, he even took out a contract on Toni’s life—a betrayal he regrets and is trying to smooth over to no avail. Hence his interest in Cliff: Rather than hiring him as a stuntman for one of his swaggering action pictures, he wants the man who once reputedly took down Bruce Lee to act as messenger—and muscle—in a hand-off with the Verduscos, and if Cliff can save him some money as a negotiator, all the better. For Cliff, it’s not just about getting paid, but acting as a white knight for Toni, positioned provocatively by the script as possible analogue to Sharon—an equivalency that Tarantino and Fincher ram home during another startling, nighttime Hollywood Hills home invasion sequence designed to showcase the Verduscos’ ruthlessness. And then some: The disparity between the largely laid-back vibes of so much of The Further Mis-Adventures and the straight up horror of its most violent scene could be taken for a wild fluctuation in tone; another way to look at it, though, is as an assertion of nerve. When Tarantino does violence, it’s generally wild, cathartic, and cartoony—like Christoph Waltz says before unloading on Leo DiCaprio in Django Unchained, he can’t help himself. Fincher, though, likes to put the sting in death, and to give the maker of Se7en and Zodiac the benefit of the doubt, the gratuitousness of the imagery, heightened by the fact that the Verduscos’ victims aren’t connected to the main story, but collateral damage in a sidequest with its own unknown parameters, proves devastatingly effective. Where Tarantino likes to use violence to rewrite history on a grand scale, Fincher’s movies simply take place in a violent world, and the choice to keep the Verduscos lurking on the margins of the action—without a clear idea of how or when they’ll intersect with, or meet their match in, Cliff—is inspired. (It also takes the movie into potentially dicey racial territory, although Tarantino is a savvy enough basterd to link Cliff’s service history in the Philippines to his new adversaries.) The introduction of some real bad guys suits not only Fincher’s knack for slow-burning dread but his unerring mastery of convergence: over time; on his watch, The well-groomed shaggy dog plotline dangling from Tarantino’s leash is transformed into a snapping, cornered pitbull. The relationship between bark and bite is a Tarantino specialty, of course, and his verbosity is contagious: The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth is ultimately a movie that may be slightly more fun to talk—and think— about than it is to watch, although as 2026 epics go, its 140 minutes whiz by considerably more quickly than the three-hour Mediterranean tour of The Odyssey. The common denominator between Nolan and Fincher’s films is their shared focus on storytelling, and more specifically, the realization that certain narratives—about heroism, war, showbiz, or all three—ultimately have a shelf life that catches up with them with a vengeance. Once upon a time in Hollywood, Pitt played Achilles as a petulant golden boy; he’s much better aged into his signature beach-bum Odysseus role, a man of constant sorrow hidden behind a devilish (or demonic) grin, and if there’s any pathos amidst the playful sadism of The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth, it lies in the question of where a cipher who’s spent his life servicing alpha-male fantasies goes after the final reel. Maybe straight to Hell—Tarantino loves his all-immolating infernos—and maybe Heaven (aka Cielo Drive). With its endless, archaic allusions to network primetime lineups, below-the-line-ephemera, abrupt narrative detours, and relentless top-down flexes of streaming-giant power, Fincher and Tarantino have crafted a charmingly unpleasant (or unpleasantly charming) fable that many people won’t enjoy as much they’d hoped—and which maybe only a film critic could love (or deconstruct, which is the same thing). Whether or not it means shit to them is hard to say, but their hearts—such as they are, black and bleeding and beating—are in it all the same.
