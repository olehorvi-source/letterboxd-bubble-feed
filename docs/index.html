2026-09-06 Possible Love (2026) davidehrlich secondary 4.5 no Another heart-stopping and novelistically layered stunner from modern cinema’s most literary auteur (a fiction author himself), Lee Chang-dong’s “Possible Love” might be dedicated to the memory of Krzysztof Kieślowski’s “Dekalog 10,” but let’s dispel any notion of it being a remake. Conceived independently, and then lumped during pre-production into the same tribute omnibus that kicked off with Asghar Farhadi’s dreadful “Parallel Tales” at Cannes this year this film shares nothing in common with the final episode of Kieślowski’s series beyond the biblical commandment that Lee splays on screen at the very start: “Thou shalt not covet.” And even then, the evidence of that instruction proves elusive in this sprawling drama about two very different couples who are brought together by the violent suppression of a workers’ strike at a major Korean metals factory. Who is coveting what, exactly? It’s simple enough to understand what the film’s characters want on the surface — or at least that’s true of its women. A casually rich dilettante whose pale skin glows with the same lustrousness as her salon-perfect bob, first-time director Ye-ji (Cho Yeo-jeong, similarly cast as the rich mom in “Parasite”) hopes to make a documentary about the declining state of labor across South Korea, with a particular focus on the recent unrest at a business called SP Heavy Industries. Although we see Ye-ji pitching the project to a grant board of some kind, the presumption is that most of her funding — and all of her creative freedom — has been provided by her imperiously chiseled husband Sang-woo (Lee newcomer Zo In-sung), a trust fund type who appears to enjoy a rather frictionless existence. On the other side of the economic divide, the plucky but sad-eyed Mi-ok (played by “Secret Sunshine” actress Jeon Do-yeon, who delivers another once-a-century performance less than 20 years since her first collaboration with Lee) is clearly willing to do whatever it takes to bring her husband back to life. One of the “dead” who lost his job and sense of purpose in the mass layoffs at SP two years ago, her taciturn and implosive Ho-seok (“Peppermint Candy” star Sul Kyung-gu) has been a shell of himself since being injured amid the crackdown that ended the ensuing strike, and Mi-ok hopes that participating in Ye-ji’s doc might allow him to process his trauma — to stop shuffling around like a zombie and snap back into the man she once fell in love with as a teenage seamstress who didn’t have any better options. That he might become a more present father to their young son Cheol, whose current rudderlessness is best expressed by his abject incompetence at Taekwondo. Beyond that simple baseline, the matrix of desire at work in Lee and Oh Jung-mi’s remarkably nuanced script is too slippery to pin down until the movie’s velvet gutpunch of a final scene. Yes, Mi-ok lusts after Ye-ji’s elegant wardrobe and designer handbags, and there’s an unspoken sexual charge in the air whenever she’s alone with Sang-woo (a charge that eventually sets fire to an unforgettable pre-dawn sequence on a private beach in the third act). But it would be unwisely reductive to conclude that the working-class woman is jealous of her hyper-affluent new bestie, just as it would be unhelpfully rash to assume that envy plays a more pivotal role in this story than resentment, or that a film as mottled and probing as “Possible Love” might be content to retrench the divide between Korea’s haves and have-nots. ~this review continues on IndieWire~

(1968) thejoshl primary 3.0 no One of the weirder and funnier sexploitation I’ve seen, in part because Russ Meyer appears to be making a pointed satire of the types of inscrutable melodrama and sexual encounters required of him when writing things like “plot” or “character” in a film like this, but also tries to throw in some I think earnest hot button 1968 political commentary (on Vietnam, communism, civil rights, the sexual revolution, etc) that interacts with all the comedy and horniness in a fashion I’ll generously describe as confused. I knew what I was in for tonally when this opened on a British Columbia travel montage where the generically handsome, borderline Sirkian pilot leading man is described by the narrator as flying through “BUSH country!” (Emphasis and all.) And yet Erica Gavin’s lead performance as the titular nymphomaniac housewife who seduces nearly every character in the film (a local Canadian Mountie, a pair of husband-and-wife tourists on their way to Toronto, her Nazi insginia attired denim biker “baby brother”!!) primarily by rubbing her topless self on them, doing a striptease dance with fish, or being a bully/insane racist still managed to shock me a few times. Wasn’t really sure what to make of the draft dodging black man vs. Irish IRA Marxist plane-hijacking piracy finale (an insane sentence to write out about a Russ Meyer skin flick), but there was enough in here to be entertained and baffled by that I was never bored. And whatever you think of the characters and performances in his movies, you could never accuse Meyer of being anything but inventive when it comes to composing shots and cutting together a manic montage. That low-angle shot up Gavin’s dress while she does the fish dance, the one between her legs as she prepares to seduce a fellow housewife (laughed out loud that they appear to have sex by rubbing their breasts against each other—dude couldn’t contain himself!), the under the bed spring POV shot as her brother gets on top of her, the multiple instances of cross-cutting between Vixen in the thralls of sexual ecstasy while other characters try and fail to have serious conversations about their marriage or geopolitics. 2026-09-06 Making Marie Antoinette (2026) davidehrlich secondary no The term “nepo baby” had yet to be coined by the time that Sofia Coppola — prematurely defined by her punchline of a performance in daddy Francis’ “The Godfather Part III” — re-emerged in her late 20s as one of the most gifted filmmakers of her generation, but few celebrities in her position, before or since, have ever been so acutely responsive to how they’re seen in the public eye. Drawing from lived experience in a way that allowed her to interpolate the biases she’d faced as both a Hollywood scion and a young woman in the arts, Coppola’s first two features directly confronted the sort of privilege and presumption that had shadowed her since childhood. Each in their own ways, “The Virgin Suicides” and “Lost in Translation” vividly traced the interior lives of girls who weren’t permitted to have them, and they did so with a hyper-real sensitivity that allowed the movies to resonate with anyone who’s ever felt observed but unseen, as if just out of frame in every picture that had ever been taken of them. Of course, success can — and often should — breed a special kind of resentment for nepo babies who dare to make a name for themselves, and so it was no surprise that people only sharpened their knives from “dinner” to “butcher” after Coppola won the Oscar for Best Original Screenplay in 2004. By the same token, it was, perhaps, even less of a surprise that Coppola’s response to such bitterness was to make a movie humanizing Marie Antoinette, the one-time Queen of France and eternal figurehead of unearned entitlement (“I know I will be able to express how a girl experiences the grandeur of a palace,” Coppola wrote to Antoinette biographer Antonia Fraser, whose book she hoped to use as source material). Least surprising of all? That a sizable percentage of critics and audiences were rather ungenerous towards the finished product, a $40 million costume drama more interested in the rhythms, customs, and furnishings of Antoinette’s gilded cage than it is in the anger her reign helped to foment beyond the palace walls. Never mind that Antoinette was a brilliantly natural subject for a filmmaker so compelled by the negative space between women and the worlds they inhabit, or that the movie’s panopticon-like vision of Versailles allowed Coppola to explore the relationship between power and personhood with peerless clarity, or that her gauzy direction invited the movie to love Marie Antoinette without ever letting her off the hook — or out of the guillotine, as it were (talk about having your cake and eating it too). Never mind that Coppola shrewdly offset her production’s unprecedented access to the actual palace grounds with a variety of inspired anachronisms (chief among them the single greatest soundtrack of the 21st century), and as a result created a sharply modern look at the unbearable lightness of being famous. Never mind!! Not that I was mad about that. Please don’t put in the newspaper that I got mad. Time has been kind to people who share my view that “Marie Antoinette” is a semi-understood masterpiece, and not only because critics like me have been increasingly emboldened to argue that case. (In 2022, I included it on my Sight & Sound ballot of the 10 greatest films ever made). Following years of murmured reconsideration, the film will be formally canonized into The Criterion Collection this November. And before that, MUBI will release — first in theaters, and then on its streaming platform — a feature-length documentary about the making of “Marie Antoinette” that is called, if you can imagine, “Making Marie Antoinette.” Despite what the 600-word preamble you just read might have led you to believe, that documentary is the true subject of this article. Sorry. But we all went on that long walk together for two reasons: 1.) I am physically and spiritually incapable of passing up any chance to get on my soapbox about the films of Sofia Coppola, whose formative impact on me would be impossible to overstate, and 2.) Because anyone reading this review of “Making Marie Antoinette” should know that I clicked my screener link for this movie like I was opening the fucking Ark of the Covenant. Footage of Rip Torn loosening up before a banquet scene, or of Jason Schwartzman trying to make Kirsten Dunst laugh before they get in the royal bed together… To some people, this might be the routine stuff of a vintage EPK, but to me it’s like watching DV video of Michelangelo painting the Sistine Chapel. ~this review continues on IndieWire~
