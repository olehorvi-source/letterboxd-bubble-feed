2026-10-31 The Devils (1971) thejoshl primary 5.0 yes “I have been a man. I have loved women. I have enjoyed power." “Do you love the Church?” “Not today.” Even watching a crummy DVD rip 8 years ago when I first started podcasting about movies like this, it was already clear that Ken Russell’s violently hysterical, feverishly cinematic realization of Aldous Huxley’s non-fiction accounting + John Whiting’s west end theatre adaptation of French Catholic priest Urbain Grandier’s burning at the stake for the crime of rebelling against religious celibacy and allegedly seducing a convent of Ursuline nuns (which was subsequently deemed an act of satanic possession by corrupt church and state figures politically motivated to scapegoat and destroy him) was an unsung masterpiece unfairly buried and lost to time due to a conservative censorship campaign and outdated accusations of sacrilegious pornography. And if you would’ve told me then that I would eventually see this in its fully restored, uncut X-rated glory on a film print in my lifetime I wouldn’t have believed you yet here we are… What a surreal yet appropriate way to spend Devil’s Night. Russell certainly courted many of the controversies he suffered by stylishly leaning into the grotesque obscenity of his rendering of 17th century France, contrasting the gorgeous decadent historical production design with the rotten reality of this plague-ridden environment of mass graves and oppressive religious hypocrisy. A contrast that also finds a home in the psychological and political duel between Oliver Reed’s handsome, charismatic and womanizing Father Grandier (“a man worth going to hell for”) whose desire for the church to recognize sexual desire as natural is recognized as both technically progressive yet also sinfully self-serving, and Vanessa Redgrave’s deeply lonely, repressed and resentful hunchbacked nun Sister Jeanne, whose psychosexual attraction to Grandier spirals into gruesome fetishization of Catholic suffering/torture/martyrdom and eventually, inadvertently becomes a cynically manipulated weapon for the consolidation of state power. It isn’t particularly original or subtle in its agenda to deconstruct the machinations of how fascists have historically demonized popular figures who threaten their authority, but the delirious, frenzied and mesmerizingly hellish manner it goes about expressing it is the source of its uniquely nauseating power. It’s the surreal dreaminess of how it depicts Sister Jeanne’s psychosexual obsession with Grandier (envision him in place of a crucified Jesus, erotically licking his wounds), the irreverent debauched mania of its infamous “Rape of Christ” orgy sequence, the overwhelming lavish filthiness and noisy circus made of this obvious scheme to manipulate the masses, and the unflinching commitment to seeing through vivid brutality of Grandier’s humiliating, leg-shattering, fiery Passion of Joan of Arc punishment. By the time Sister Jeanne is upsettingly, pathetically masturbating with the charred femur of the man she so intensely lusted for, the only response at that point is uncomfortable, exhausted laughter at the sheer sweeping theatricality and morbidly horrific degradation on display. Whether that gaudy, maximalist approach to historical and spiritual subject matter usually handled with elegance and austerity is productive or exploitative was part of a lot of critic’s frustrations with it back in 1971, but decades later there’s something to be said about passionate moral outrage deliberately, ostentatiously conveyed in such an arguably immoral, outrageous fashion. “Call me vain and proud, the greatest sinner ever to walk God's earth. But Satan's boy I could never be. I haven't the humility.” [ 35mm ] Full discussion on ep 30 of my podcast SLEAZOIDS .

2026-10-30 Who Killed Alex Odeh? (2026) davidehrlich secondary 3.0 no Times change, the arc of the moral universe bends towards justice, and yet one thing that has remained true for as long as I’ve been alive is that prejudice, bigotry, and explicit displays of racism have always been openly permissible in America — so long as they’ve been targeted at Arabs and/or Muslims. That sad and immutable fact was on full display throughout Zohran Mamdani’s mayoral campaign last year, and the say the quiet part out loud reaction to Abdul El-Sayed’s current senate race has somehow managed to be even worse on both sides of the aisle (Democrats lament that he “doesn’t share our values,” while Republicans straight up just call the guy a jihadist). But anyone old enough to remember 9/11 knows that it didn’t start with the likes of Vicky Paladino, just as I assume that anyone old enough to remember the assassination of Alex Odeh knows that it didn’t start with 9/11. The sole fatality of a bomb that had been planted at the California office where he served as the West Coast Regional Director of the American-Arab Anti-Discrimination Committee (ADC), Alexander Michel Odeh was murdered on the morning of October 11, 1985, leaving behind his wife Norma and their three young daughters Helena, Samya, and Susan. To this day, his slaughter remains “unsolved.” And yet, as Jason Osder and William Youmans’ scattered but vital documentary “Who Killed Alex Odeh?” makes clear soon after it begins, there’s little doubt as to who was behind the killing. Indeed, the film establishes that the case was effectively solved within days of the attack, and that all three of the primary suspects — all members of a right-wing terrorist group known as the Jewish Defense League — were only allowed to flee to Israel because powerful forces within the American political system preferred for them to get away with it. And to get away with it. And to get away with it. More than 40 years later, no efforts have been made to prosecute or extradite the presumptive murderers. One of them has even moved back to the United States. And so, despite affecting the aesthetic and tropes of the true-crime format, “Who Killed Alex Odeh?” directly contradicts the expectations of its supposed genre. Instead of establishing a vast mystery for their movie to explore, Osder and Youmans — one of them Jewish, the other a Palestinian-American — give shape to the galling absence of one. Stunted and searching by design, their film unpacks the sociopolitical context of Odeh’s killing in order to illustrate how the easily Google-able question posed by its title could possibly remain “unanswered” for so long, and even partakes in some light subterfuge as part of an effort to pin down the culprits on camera. ~this review continues on IndieWire~
