2026-10-27 The Mosquito Bowl (2026) davidehrlich secondary 2.0 no There have been any number of movies about the hell of fighting in World War II, the most visceral of which have been made in the years since “Saving Private Ryan” provided a very enduring template for how modern filmmakers could render that carnage with both the spectacle of a blockbuster and the horror of a memory. Peter Berg’s brutal and concussive “The Mosquito Bowl” is definitely one of them! No one could ever say that it’s not. What differentiates this unsparing epic from the scores of other stories about the Greatest Generation, however, is that some of its characters are good at football. Indeed, these All-American college boys are so good at football that, on Christmas Eve 1944, the soldiers are ordered to play a morale-boosting game of two-hand touch on the beach where their units are preparing for the Battle of Okinawa. And for the span of the eight-minute montage during which “The Mosquito Bowl” actually makes good on its title, it’s easy to picture how the famous scrimmage — fought between a roster of impossibly beautiful athletes in their primes — might have been used to backstop a story that accentuated the senseless waste of sending thousands of young men to their deaths. Alas, for the rest of this 126-minute film, that waste is better expressed through Berg’s failure to make anything more of their lives, as the “Spenser Confidential” auteur’s latest Netflix project does almost nothing else to distinguish itself from a genre so well-worn that it’s seemingly run out of ways to blow up a human body. Why “almost”? I was hoping you wouldn’t ask. For starters, there’s the decision to cast thin-skinned ESPN commentator Pat McAfee as an R. Lee Ermey-inspired drill sergeant. “The Mosquito Bowl” also stands out for how confusingly it conflates the thrill and teamwork of combat sport with the violence and camaraderie of armed conflict; war is presented as both a natural extension of what these boys have been trained to do on the gridiron (to the point that the soldiers briefly diagram their plan of attack as if it were a football play), and also the catastrophe that robbed them of a more innocent purpose. (Berg loves the troops as much as he hates the fact that generals and presidents so wantonly sacrifice their lives.) Someone declares that “The Marine Corps is the greatest offensive line in history,” and yet the film’s characters don’t seem to have any special advantages on the field of battle, as it would be glib to suggest that even the country’s best college running back might be able to slip free of a mortar blast. It’s one thing to argue that football is war, and another, much more asinine thing to argue that war is football. ~this review continues on IndieWire~

2026-10-27 Jason X (2001) thejoshl primary 3.0 no Not sure I can intellectually explain or justify why I am far more charmed by the qualities that make the New Line entries in this series "bad" than I am the ones that made the Paramount ones so. I guess maybe it comes down to the fact that I think the flaws of these films stem more from misguided over-ambition and/or deliberate camp rather than simply boring laziness. Regardless, this is somehow the most expensive Friday the 13th movie and yet easily looks the cheapest, in part due to the fact that creature FX artist turned director James Isaac (whose work on The Fly , Naked Lunch and eXistenZ resulted in the delightful Cronenberg cameo) was attempting to take Jason into the world of claustrophobic spaceship horror a la Alien and only had the requisite skill and resources to get to SyFy original Star Trek knock-off. It makes a lot of sense that multiple Canadian cast members went on to be in Andromeda ! Despite this hilariously falling short of what the plan clearly was, there’s an endearing stupidity to it all. Whether it’s the details of how cryogenically frozen lab experiment Jason is brought aboard the ship (after escaping his restraints and impaling Dr. David Cronenberg!), or the nonsensical details of this futuristic Earth II space expedition crew that despite being astronauts/scientists are just as horny and scantily clad as any nubile 80s camp counsellors, or the tonally baffling killing spree setpieces that flip on a dime between being legitimately gross to winking slapstick. Ultimately, I had a good time watching my hulking buddy smash liquid nitrogen heads off countertops, wield a giant machete-sized surgical tool through the chromatic sets, gruesomely spin laser tag armor attired military grunts down corkscrews, fight and have his limbs/head blown off by sexy dominatrix androids (that dual-wield machine pistols and know kung fu—like Milla in PWSA’s Resident Evil movies), go metallic cyborg “Uber-Jason” mode and get tricked by artificial VR holograms of teen campers going “we love premarital sex!” Him beating them to death in their sleeping bags felt like a return to tradition moment surrounded by all this uncanny modernity. 400 years in the future, it’s very possible we might become civilized enough to ban hockey, but we will still be wearing cropped shirts and clamping nipples, and thus we will always need Jason to wake up and put us in our place. Green-screen Kane Hodder weightlessly floating on wires away from CGI explosion in space just a beautifully ugly image I never thought I’d see when I began watching these humble no-budget camp serial killer movies. Also, not generally a fan of writers trying to copycat the Kevin Williamson post-modern Scream dialogue (honestly I barely tolerate him doing it at times), but must confess that “It’s okay guys, he just wanted his machete back!”, “This sucks on so many levels” and “It's gonna take more than a poke in the ribs to put down this old dog… Yeah, that oughta do it...” all got me good.
