2026-08-20 High-Rise (2015) brofromanother primary yes *My 2017 Ben Wheatley monograph is out of print; here's a chapter because why not?* Originally published as a journal entry in 1901 before being released as a book three years later, Sigmund Freud's The Psychopathology of Everyday Life is widely considered to be one of its author's major works -- a meditation on aberrance and neurosis that concludes, in the words of its original American translator, A.A. Brill, that the “hitherto considered impassable gap between normal and abnormal mental states is more apparent than real.” For Freud, common and supposedly minor deviations from average civilized conduct, like the temporary forgetting of proper names, were signs of deeper drives thinly cloaked by learned social etiquette. Furthermore, he believed that these impulses, and the taboo yearnings attached to them, would never truly degrade or disappear over time. “The unconscious,” he writes, “knows no time limit... the most important as well as the most peculiar character of psychic fixation...consists in the fact that all impressions are on the one hand retained in the same form as they were received, and also in the forms that they have assumed in their further development.” In other words, when it comes to human behavior, and also the civilizations that grow out of it, what's past is not prologue. It is, rather, an irrepressible force, reasserting itself throughout time, over and over again. The characters in J.G. Ballard's 1975 novel High-Rise experience similar feelings of displacement as they crouch together in the ruins of what was once a luxury apartment block and now more accurately resembles a network of dark caves. By acting out their most deeply buried fantasies – from rape and revenge to tribal warfare --the high rise dwellers have willed themselves back into primitive state. In the wreckage, the post-apocalyptic merges with the pre-historic, and time and civilization folds in on themselves. “Even the run-down nature of the high-rise was a model of the world of the world into which the future was carrying them,” writes Ballard near the book's climax. “Sometimes [they] found it difficult not to believe that they were living in a future that had already taken place.” Like Freud, Ballard was preoccupied with impulses and the architecture of the collective unconscious; taken together, his novels and short stories carve out a place at the intersection of science-fiction and behavioral analysis. While he primarily wrote fiction, Ballard’s formal training as a doctor – including an early ambition to pursue psychiatry – introduced a clinical element into his prose style. Shifting in his early twenties from copy-writing to genre short-stories (a fascination prompted by a move to North America and immersion in its burgeoning pop-cultural industries), Ballard pioneered and all but patented his own form of future-shock horror in novels like Crash, The Drowned World and High-Rise, to the point that “Ballardian” has become a recognized adjective in the Collins English Dictionary: “resembling or suggestive of the conditions described in Ballard’s novels & stories, esp. dystopian modernity, bleak man-made landscapes & the psychological effects of technological, social or environmental developments.” Ballard would return to these tropes regularly over the years, always finding variations on the theme. “In [Ballard's] view, “writes John Gray, “much of history is a succession of episodes of elective mass insanity, with whole populations opting for psychosis as a way of coping with otherwise intolerable realities.” The bleak, man-made landscape in High-Rise is the forty-story tower of the title, “a small vertical city, its two thousand inhabitants boxed up into the sky.” The mass insanity, meanwhile, takes hold from the novel's first line, which takes its place as one of the great literary cold opens: “later, as he sat on his balcony eating the dog, Dr. Robert Laing reflected on the unusual events that had taken place within this huge apartment building during the previous three months.” In the space of a single, elegantly unsettling sentence, Ballard introduces and distills the idea of human beings reverting to base savagery against a high-tech backdrop: a vivid variation on the inexorably regressive, anti-social behavior that intrigued Freud seventy-five years earlier. A quick insert shot lodged in the middle of the opening montage of the film adaptation of High-Rise cinches the connection between Freud and Ballard. We see one of Dr. Laing's fellow tenants strolling through the lobby on his way to the parking lot with his nose buried deeply in a book: a paperback edition of The Psychopathology of Everyday Life. Such playfulness is common in Wheatley's film, which takes several irreverent liberties with its source material. “Where Ballard is cold and precise, Wheatley is messy and excessive,” writes Adam Lowenstein, sounding a familiar note in High-Rise’s early reception: that Wheatley's aggressively psychedelic approach to the material undermines the spare power of the novel. But for all its deviations, High-Rise still feels like a faithfully Ballardian work – a film that feels close to its author's obsessions, and which honors his writing’s uncanny prescience. Any discussion of the complex play of fidelity and invention in High-Rise must begin with the screenplay, which was written solely by Amy Jump (the second Wheatley film in a row following A Field in England to be so signed). Jump retains the book's basic narrative outline: the arrival at the high-rise of bachelor Dr. Laing (Tom Hiddleston) and the subsequent, inexplicable descent into madness of all of the tenants --Laing included – culminating in the assassination of the building's architect and then the murder of his killer. Jump transfers large swaths of Ballard's text into a voice-over delivered by Laing in the third person, which lines up with both the detachment of Ballard's literary voice and the character's allusive surname. Ballard was surely thinking of R.D. Laing, author of The Divided Self, a writer who saw himself as Freud's successor even as he inveighed against clinical diagnoses of psychological disorders. A self-styled “anti-psychiatrist,” Laing viewed mental illness as a potentially transformative process, and a valid expression of personal or emotional distress –psychosis as a social coping mechanism. By showing the character suffering from symptoms of dissociative disorder – i.e. talking to himself about himself -- Wheatley and Jump further entrench the reference, aided by their Hiddleston’s effectively detached performance, which mostly eschews the easy movie-star charm of his other work. It’s too simple to say that the actor empties himself out to play Laing; it’s more that he finds a way to inhabit a set of inchoate longings beneath an immaculate surface, effectively linking the character to the building even before either has begun their descent. Laing’s arrival high-rise is presented in a stately montage at the outset of a film-long flashback, Jump having followed Ballard by beginning at the end, with the consumption of a barbecued Alsatian. The film’s continued focus on his story slightly upends Ballard's tripartite structure, which balances the doctor's experiences amongst the high-rise's crumbling upper middle class (he lives on the 25th floor) with the high and low ends represented by his fellow tenants Anthony Royal and Richard Wilder, who, as their Dickensian monikers suggest, occupy opposite points along the spectrum. Respectively, they are the building's decadent architect and its most dangerously dissatisfied lower-floor resident, and the dynamic between Royal's diffident supremacy (he dresses in all white and walks with a cane), Wilder's defiant aggressiveness (he's described as a barrel-chested alpha male) and Laing's paralyzed passivity provides High-Rise with a psychological dynamism, –a triangulation encompassing super-ego, id and ego. Ultimately, the high-rise definitively imposes its will on these three differently archetypal men – and the other people in their respective orbits – until their individual personalities have been whittled away, leaving only a series of mutually shared characteristics reflecting feral, base desires. The joke is that the high-rise finally betrays its architect's carefully codified construction (and its financiers' class-based pricing system) by turning its inhabitants into equals, of a sort, with disintegration and death as the great levelers. Jump's screenplay places this strange egalitarianism front and center, transposing one of Laing's observations from the latter half of the book into the very first scene and firmly setting the film's tone as boldly, cynically comic: “for all its inconveniences, Laing was satisfied with life in the high-rise.” Producer Jeremy Thomas had previously optioned High-Rise for Nicolas Roeg in the late 1970s, leading to screenplays by Paul Mayersberg and Rudy Wurlitzer that were never produced. After working with David Cronenberg on another Ballard adaptation, Crash (1996), Thomas brought High-Rise to Canadian director Vincenzo Natali, who got a script draft by Richard Stanley, but this too fell by the wayside. Through it all, Thomas retained the rights and eventually met up with Wheatley, whose work he admired: “I had liked Sightseers so much I had seen it twice,” he told Deadline Hollywood. “Ben’s idea was to set it when it was written, in 1975, on the cusp of Thatcher coming to power. I realized I been going up the wrong track with it by trying to set it in the present when actually it was more contemporary by setting it in that period.” Wheatley and Jump's decision is daring insofar as it inverts the angle of Ballard's allegory to an almost counterintuitive degree. When the book was written, its vision of a massive apartment complex “colonizing the sky,” and offering gilded amenities to anybody wealthy enough to buy in was prescient. In retrospect, this prediction appears merely inevitable – science-fiction as everyday reality. “In 2014, high-rise living is no longer a novelty in the UK,” writes Ned Bauman in an introduction to a new edition of High Rise. “Londoners will read the book beneath a skyline sheenier than ever with notched glass, 500-foot test tubes ready for the admixture of volatile chemicals.” In the last few years alone, several genre films have used brutalist structures in a quasi- Ballardian manner. Both Joe Cornish's Attack the Block and Ciaran Foy's Citadel (2012) imagine UK tower blocks forcibly overrun by monstrous creatures, and there are also (surely unconscious echoes) of High-Rise in Andrea Arnold’s surveillance-themed thriller Red Road (2005) and Gareth Evans' more action-oriented The Raid (2011), whose policeman hero must scale a gigantic, gangster-infested apartment complex in Jakarta. Like The Raid, High-Rise mixes CGI exteriors with real locations; it was shot in an abandoned structure in Northern Ireland, with the outdoor shots devised and executed by the UK effects house MilkVFX. “From the outside, the towers are impressively monstrous, the angular break of their upper floors turning them into giant lizards craning at the sky,” wrote Mark Kermode in his review in The Observer, and the vague anthropomorphization of the building he notes is in line with Ballard's own writing – he describes the building as a monster that “seemed almost to challenge the sun itself.” The shift from exterior locations in Sightseers and A Field in England is significant, but High-Rise is still not quite an urban film. Ballard writes that “the office buildings of central London belonged to a different world, in time as well as in space,” and so Wheatley's high-rise is isolated away from the city, while the sound design places it strangely in a pastoral context. When the characters are on their balconies, the soundtrack is dominated by seagull calls – signifiers of the natural world that the building seeks to shut out and repress. Of course, the paradox of Ballard's story is that the building is also a massively scaled incubator for animalistic behavior: nesting and aggressive territoriality. Space is always being contested in High-Rise: a bird's eye view of the high-rise parking lot takes in two cars vying for the same empty spot, a high-speed game of one-upsmanship that ends, as it must, with one vehicle cutting the other off and claiming the narrow patch of turf. When the driver emerges, however, he's in full 18th Century regalia, striding into the building in the manner of a victory-lapping aristocrat. This incongruous sight gag pays off a few scenes later when Laing is summoned to a party at Royal's apartment, where the revelers are all similarly arrayed in anachronistic finery – powdered wigs and waistcoats. Here, Wheatley and Jump are playing up the class-warfare aspect of High-Rise to an absurd level, an overtness also informs the depiction of Royal's rooftop garden, a space that has no direct equivalent in the book, and serves here to bring the material closer to the baroquely art-directed fantasias of Terry Gilliam or Jeunet and Caro, or the bourgie Vortex of John Boorman’s Zardoz (1974), one of Wheatley's favorite movies, and itself a stylized parable of haves and have-nots that concludes with the barbarians breaking through the gate and massacring the effete cadre of intellectuals that sought to repress them. (Boorman’s arsenal of in-camera optical effects ranging from super-impositions to screens-within-screens, seems a plausible aesthetic influence; a recent Blu-Ray edition of the film features a long encomium to Zarrdoz’s greatness from Wheatley, who saw it for the first time on television). “Turn the Snowpiercer train from a horizontal to a vertical axis, and this is what it looks like,” observed Tom Charity in Cinema Scope, noting the similarities between High-Rise and South Korean genre specialist Bong Joon-ho’s 2014 sci-fi thriller Snowpiercer, in which the last vestiges of humanity fight for scraps and carry out raids within the space of a high-speed locomotive racing around the globe on a single track. (The film is based on a French graphic novel published seven years after High-Rise). Where Bong's near-futuristic setting allows him and his collaborators to imagine a world different from our own – a wasteland suggesting Mad Max in deep freeze -- Wheatley is at once liberated and constrained by what Charity goes on to refer to as “the “delightfully oxymoronic possibilities of an apocalyptic period film...[High Rise] filters memories of ’70s Britain through rancorous satire: desperately bourgeois wife-swapping parties, seething class conflict, and mounting piles of rubbish.” Charity's analysis is echoed by Wheatley's own comments in The Guardian on the eve of production, which played up the idea of High-Rise as a movie imbued with an element of nostalgia. "I was born in 1972, three years before the book was written, so one of the attractions of the film was that I kind of imagine myself as one of the kids running around on the estate and my parents as the adults.” This comment accounts for the increased presence in Jump's screenplay of Toby (Louis Suc), the young son of Charlotte Melville (Sienna Miller), a high-rise tenant who becomes Laing's lover early on in the story. In the book, Charlotte's son goes unnamed and is only glimpsed on the periphery of the action, but Wheatley and Jump privilege his point of view on several occasions; during a bacchanal at Charlotte's apartment, we see him peering curiously out of his bedroom door at the grown-ups splaying out drunkenly on couches and beneath tables. Later, we see Toby in his room working on a home-made kaleidoscope, which he tells Laing “can see the future,” a detail that proves significant as the film progresses. In Sight and Sound, Henry K. Miller sees these scenes as the director tipping his hand; “the adoption of Toby’s point of view [is] also perhaps the view of child-of- the-1970s Wheatley.” From this perspective, High-Rise follows Steven Spielberg's Empire of the Sun (1987), which adapted Ballard's semi-autobiographical novel about life in a Japanese military prison camp in Shanghai during World War II into a child's-eye epic, with ten-year old Jim (Christian Bale) studiously absorbing the horror around him. Wheatley is of course not a remotely Spielbergian filmmaker; his style is too fast and relentless for the sense of awe that radiates at at all times from the maker of Close Encounters of the Third Kind (1977) and E.T. (1982), and there is no place for true innocence in his universe. Nor is there much room for the cool clinicism of David Cronenberg, who made his own – completely providential -- version of High-Rise in 1975 under the title of Shivers; the coincidence of a low-budget Canadian horror film about an outbreak of sexual hysteria in a state-of-the-art apartment complex (in Montreal) in the same year that Ballard published his novel must be taken in stride, but Cronenberg’s decision to make a film version of Crash two decades later confirmed him as one of the writer’s acolytes (not surprisingly, Cronenberg is also very interested in Freud, invoking him in a Grand Guignol context in The Brood (1979) and then having Viggo Mortensen play him, dangling cigar and all, in the historical drama A Dangerous Method (2009). Beyond the casting of Cronenberg alumni Jeremy Irons, the Canadian master’s operating-theatre-aesthetics are only really present in scene set at a medical college where Laing – not yet under the high-rise's spell – dissects a human skull for a group of students. Cronenberg would surely approve of the shots of a scalpel slicing cleanly through a cadaver's head, permitting Laing to deftly peel skin from bone (“the facial mask simply slips off the skull”). But there's also some defiant self-citation here as well. When Laing moves on to a saw and then a chisel, the blunt force blows – and attendant revulsion of the onlookers – winkingly recalls the head-splitting scene from Kill List – another variation on “hammer horror.” These shots recur in a late montage where the slicing and cracking of the skull is inter-cut in rapid succession with shots of a wooden door being smashed in, and Laing pulling his dress shirt on, graphic matches respectively indicating acts of destruction (the violent collapse of boundaries between the tenants) and disguise. Having shown how easily the mask can be slipped off, Laing is shown putting it back on, in a fashion. Wheatley's love of Roeg finds its fullest flower in High Rise, including a subtle snatch of Don’t Look Now when Laing and Charlotte have sex on the latter’s balcony (a shot of Laing getting dressed again spliced in before they go at it). More than its predecessors, High-Rise is structured around montages, many of them debaucherous party sequences: dancing, drugging, and general mayhem abound. For Slant's Chuck Bowen, the “orgies and violence... afford Wheatley the opportunity to indulge in all manners of aesthetic overkill: slow-motion, strobe lights, multi-colored screen filters, and so on.” Bowen is not being complimentary, and his review echoes numerous other complaints that High-Rise is an exercise in over-indulgence, and that Wheatley has run roughshod over Ballard's text, swapping out its insidious minimalism for sensory overload. There is surely something monotonous about High-Rise, a sense of torpor that sets in early and is never quite alleviated. It's equally undeniable that this is by design. When Laing tells Royal (Jeremy Irons) that his blueprint of the high-rise looks like “the unconscious diagram of some kind of psychic event,” he's describing the drawing but also the shape of the film around him; both can be seen as a series of concentric circles, loops within loops, spiraling not out of control, but rather within a fixedly erratic pattern, as specified by their respective architects. There is a precedent for appreciating High-Rise’s defiantly abstract angle of attack. Writing about Zardoz in Film Comment in 1977, Raymond Durgnat described Boorman’s aesthetic as “inspired by the belief that plausibility matters less than piling one striking idea upon another and starting flash fires in the spectator’s imagination.” Durgnat’s admiration for Zardoz and its pscychedelic M.O. is mobilized in the context of what he calls “The Great British Phantasmagoria,” which he uses to group a number of filmmakers who made spectacular and purposeful breaks with the documentary tradition of post-World War II British filmmaking. In a perceptive feat of mapping, he briskly traces a line from the plangent expressionism of Powell and Pressburger to the erotic fantasies of Richard Lester and John Schlesinger to the full-bore mania of Ken Russell to the “delinquenty” japes of transplanted American Stanley Kubrick, with nods also to Lindsay Anderson, Nicolas Roeg and Peter Watkins before placing his cohort in conversation with the radical, apocalyptic Jean-Luc Godard of of La Chinoise (1967) and Weekend (1968). Durgnat’s survey tries to account for a splintered cinematic consciousness that was very much in vogue in the late 1970s, and which High-Rise resurrects. Beyond matching the author’s criteria for interrogating the “rivalry between mental images and reality,” its pile-up of influences (and the ideas attached to them) marks it as kind of self-contained “Great British Phantasmagoria,” albeit one that balances its stream-of-consciousness cinephilia with a deceptive rigor sidestepped by critics claiming undisciplined excess. Far from abandoning Ballard's themes of reversion and recurrence, Wheatley determinedly enshrines them in images that describe repetition (Laing pistoning back and forth on a rowing machine) or replication, like Laing reflected infinitely in an elevator's massive glass mirror. In Citizen Kane (1941) and The Lady From Shanghai (1947) Orson Welles pioneered this precise effect to express respectively the myriad public faces of a monomaniac and the untrustworthy perspectives of characters embroiled in a cat-and-mouse intrigue. Wheatley uses mirrors in a more Kubrickian manner, as emblems of schizophrenia: Hiddleston’s endlessly bisected visage comprises an allusion to his namesake’s The Divided Self. Laing is associated visually with the elevator, a mode of conveyance befitting his “upper-middle” status on the 25th floor. When he attends the costume party in Royal's penthouse, however, he's out of place in his simple tailored black suit – the payoff to the earlier sight gag of the impatient driver dressed as a full-on fop. This is where Kubrick's shadow begins to loom over High-Rise more fully than any of the other candidates from Durgnat’s Great British Phantasmagoria. Royal's party brings to mind the “Gold Room” revels of The Shining or the baroque orgy in Eyes Wide Shut (1999). with “all the best people” gathered in solidarity of maintaining a high-end status quo. In a quietly witty touch, a string quartet playing a Muzak version of Abba's “S.O.S.,” efficiently equating Top 40 with the ruling class while leaving the song's lyrics – and their underlying expression of panic and distress – as a structuring absence to be filled in later. “This movie probably owes more to Barry Lyndon to anything else, really,” Wheatley told David Fear, and indeed the visuals and atmosphere of Royal's party directly echo compositions from Kubrick's film, which was released in 1975 – the same year that Ballard wrote High-Rise. A sumptuous comedy of manners distended into serene slow motion, Barry Lyndon was Kubrick's follow-up to A Clockwork Orange (1971) a film that superficially seems closer to High-Rise in its dystopian setting and outbursts of cruel violence, as well as the theme of base human nature overwhelming socially conditioned responses. Where High Rise resembles Barry Lyndon is in its focus on characters who wish to rise above their station. Laing is associated visually with the elevator and its reflecting mirrors, which hint that his various trips up and down are on behalf of some larger cohort. One of Royal's henchman greets Laing in the stairwell with a scornful put-down: “well, if it isn't the social climber.” And yet Hiddleston's Laing is not a character who's terribly interested in upward mobility: he's content in his 25th floor allotment. The only movement he truly catalyzes is the downward plunge of his medical college subordinate Munrow (Augustus Prue), who he cruelly manipulates into committing suicide, a tense slow-motion sequence that recalls the death-by-falling in Sightseers right down to the close-up of a head being dashed in, this time against the hood of a car. That personal rivalry aside, Laing's goal is nothing more than blending in – an idea cinched by his obsessive search for a particular shade of silver paint that he uses to coat the interior of his apartment, and then his own body. In this way, Laing begins to take on the characteristics of the building, reinforcing Ballard's idea of the high-rise imposing its will on its inhabitants. (In a superb bit of prop management, the piles of boxes in Laing's apartment are styled as miniature high-rises). When thing start to fall apart, Laing’s protestations of innocence point towards a paralyzing passivity: “I don’t know anything about it…and I didn’t do it” he pleads before ducking back into his room. The real prime mover, is Wilder, whose need to scale the building and confront Royal picks up and carries the theme of class warfare, especially when he's presented by another tenant with the “key” he needs to enter Royal's apartment: a loaded gun. Wilder's literal ascent is juxtaposed against his mental and emotional regression, which is especially pronounced compared to the characters around him (although it’s a subtle point of the film’s costume design that Laing, whose pants are hiked up in the final scenes, and who throws his tie over the side of the building, looks like an over-grown nine-year old by the end). Wilder is frequently shown surrounded by children, and his wife Helen (Elizabeth Moss) views him with exasperated affection as an overgrown adolescent. “Injustice – my husband can't bear it,” she confides to Laing, a line that derives from Che Guevara's famous claim of trembling with indignation every time one was committed somewhere in the world. Later, we see that the couple has decorated their apartment with a portrait of the Argentine rebel leader, as well as a poster for Karel Reisz' 1966 rebel-without-a-clue comedy Morgan: A Suitable Case for Treatment, an anarchic paean to madness inspired in equal measures by The Communist Manifesto and The Divided Self. As High-Rise goes on, Wilder de-evolves from a frustrated would-be revolutionary and muckraker into a blustering, raging man-child serving his own solipsistic interests. At one point, Wheatley and Jump have him say his name into a tape recorder over and over (a detail drawn from the book) until it's just primitive howling, a harrowing passage recalling Jack Nicholson's animalistic break-down at the end of The Shining. Lost in a fugue state, Wilder sexually assaults Charlotte (off-screen), kicking off a late-film montage scored to Portishead's moody cover of “S.O.S,” which is High-Rise's theme song as surely as “Onward, Christian Soldiers” frames Kill List or “Tainted Love” sets up Sightseers’ curdled boy-meets-girl scenario. In an interview with Travis Elborough, Ballard said that he saw his books as “an early warning system,” and Wheatley picks up on that sense of anxiety in his soundtrack selection. [High-Rise] stretches out the song (in orchestral versions as well as Portishead’s) as one tortured temporality that encompasses the past, present, and future in equal doses, without ever belonging,” writes Adam Loewenstein, underlining the importance of the song’s repetition in a variety of guises. Following their earlier absence in the party scene, the song’s cheerfully lovelorn lyrics are re-inserted to float via vocalist Beth Gibbons’ tremulous performance over images of moral paralysis (Laing refusing to act as he hears the sounds of Charlotte’s rape) and physical and industrial decay. An obvious reference point for the contrapuntal trickery Wheatley is pulling here is the use of “Singin’ in the Rain’” in A Clockwork Orange, but where there the selection felt tied to Alex’s sick sense of showmanship, in High-Rise, the song’s tension and anxiety is clearly imposed from above– as an ethical interjection in the otherwise carnivalesque presentation. There is an element of direct address to the use of “S.O.S.”, and it extends also to the eerie shots of an unplugged, discarded television (shades of Videodrome) showing newsreader Cosgrove (Peter Fernandino) repeating “what are you doing?” over and over again – a verbal loop like Wilder’s, except that instead of desperately trying to reassert his identity in a dehumanized space, Cosgrove sounds weirdly accusatory. He could be an avatar for filmmakers trying to prod their audience’s passive enjoyment of their own ferociously unpleasant work). This tense, otherwise wordless passage concludes with Charlotte getting the last, unspoken word on her rape by serving Wilder a can of dog food, suggesting that he's finally sunk to the level of an animal. The parallel of Wilder sullenly eating dog food on the balcony and Laing's barbecuing of the Alsatian in the opening scenes is instructive. At this late juncture in the film, the divisions between the characters have been obliterated. In their ruined penthouse, Royal and his cadre have been reduced to the same scrounging, foraging existence as the lower-floor tenants. No longer insulated by wealth or status, they reveal themselves as identical to the subjects of their disdain, while Wilder's refusal to stay below indicates that he seems himself as worthy of a better life. The literal and figurative leveling of terrain calls to mind the final lines of Barry Lyndon: “good or bad, handsome or ugly, rich or poor, they are all equal now.” If High-Rise is indeed Wheatley's riff on Barry Lyndon, with Laing as a more tentatively ascendant protagonist, then Wilder could be a stand-in for Bullingdon, Barry's stepson, whose usurpious arc leads to that film's climax. Bullingdon's resentment of his step-father leads to him to challenge the older man to a duel. Faced with the grim prospect of murdering his wife's son, Barry purposefully fires into the ground, a gesture of impotence that's also an act of restraint, and which makes him one of Kubrick's only true heroes. Royal, who is not Wilder's father but claims paternity over Toby – and as the building's architect, functions symbolically as a group father figure – is unarmed, and so has no chance to duplicate Barry's self-sacrifice. The end result is the same: an avatar of the ruling class is suddenly brought low by younger man, coming up from underneath him. The filmmakers go even further by juxtaposing Wider's arrival at the the top floor with Helen giving birth in the next room. As she goes into contractions, he's shown moving through the ducts like a newborn emerging from the womb. In Down Terrace, the image of a heavily pregnant Valda killing Maggie suggested a form of renewal, while here, the new generation arrives in the shadow of violence. Wilder is killed by the members of Royal's harem, including Charlotte, but it's less an act of personal vengeance than the establishment of a new order – one where Wilder is thoroughly punished for killing the patriarch, even though he’s arguably doing the women’s dirty work for them. One of the smartest aspects of Jump’s script is how it juxtaposes the preening-peacock aspects of the high-rise’s male tenants with the quiet, industrious resourcefulness of the women, who are glimpsed organizing makeshift camps in the halls and doing their best to shield the children from what’s going on around them. At the same time, High-Rise doesn’t let its female characters off the hook; Moss has a terrifically creepy moment when Helen watches a bodice-ripping period drama on television and her eyes light up with a mixture of nostalgia for a ritualized, stereotyped upper-class past – of the kind on display in the Royal’s costume party – and also for a better life in the present tense: in the final scenes, she’s cast her lot in with Laing, a pairing which may give her a measure of control she could never exercise over Wilder. At the moment of Wilder's death, the point of view shifts to Toby, who watches the attack through his home-made kaleidoscope, raising the stakes of his early voyeurism from adults behaving badly to an act of cold-blooded murder – and giving High-Rise an ending that’s been coded into its visual DNA all along. The contrast between Wilder, who perceives reality through the lens of a movie camera – a journalist's tool – and Toby, whose kaleidoscope can “see the future,” re-codes the former's murder as a prophecy -- the angry emissary of the lower-class being carved up by the gate-keepers at the top floor. That the killers are women is a key Ballardian detail: “in their bloodied hands, they carried knives with narrow blades...shy but happy now, Wilder tottered across the roof to meet his new mothers.” It also sets up an audio snippet of Margaret Thatcher praising neoliberal economics, which serves as High-Rise's grace note. “Where where there is state capitalism, there will never be political freedom,” we hear her say, her voice presiding over the wreckage of the building – and also the larger-scale wreckage to come on her watch as Prime Minister. Toby and his kaleidoscope have seen the future after all. As of this writing, less than two months after High-Rise's theatrical release, Wheatley and Jump's choice to end their film with an audio cameo by Thatcher has come in for as much criticism as the hyperbolic visual style. Henry Miller is unimpressed by dragging on Thatcher at the end as a kind of diabolus ex machina that everyone can be expected to boo,” while Mark Blacklock calls it “apogee of the periodisation in the film...[and] it strikes a particularly unfortunate note.” Blacklock cites Ballard's – seemingly cheeky – admiration of Thatcher (“I think she exerts a powerful sexual spell, and I'm not alone”) as evidence that “[the author] would have preferred [her] as a character within the high-rise, a participant in the experiment.” This speculative observation ignores the gender politics of the film – which already mischeviously conflate maternal gentleness and brutal violence – and frankly reduces the complexity of Wheatley and Jump's coda, which does not work to simply retrospectively vilify Thatcher (an intention already borne out in a subtler way in Down Terrace, via the malevolent character of Maggie) but to acknowledge Ballard's uncanny foresight in writing and releasing the book right on the eve of her election. What Blacklock refers to as a “considerable divergence from the source text at the level of what we might want to call latent content” is merely an acknowledgment that “latency” is long beside the point in 2015, after the impact of Thatcher’s election has already been understood. Wheatley's and Jump's modified ending ambitiously – and kaleidoscopically -- refracts Ballard's conceit of a “future that had already taken place” by offering up a vision that is simultaneously impossible – the unresolvable contradiction of an “apocalyptic period piece,” that places the end of the world at some point in the past – and all too concretely realistic. “Keep the change,” Laing tells a supermarket cashier at one point; “there isn’t any,” she responds – a a rim-shot that works subliminally as a repudiation of Royal’s ambition to make the high-rise into a “crucible for change.” Far from simple redundancy, Jump’s script, with its myriad echoes and repetitions, describes a holding pattern – an unconscious diagram of a psychic event describing the inability to truly move forward despite superficial signifiers of progress. As in Down Terrace, Kill List, Sightseers and A Field in England, it’s an ending that’s also very much a beginning: like Kubrick, Wheatley and Jump are more interested in cycles of recurrence than gestures of resolution. As Toby spies a series of new building developments -- an act of bearing witness given in the book to Laing -- he's unmistakably looking forward. Wheatley, meanwhile, looks backward, towards the England of his childhood. The space between these two glances comprises an anxious, unstable present tense: S.O.S. There is another, differently troubling way to read the end of High-Rise, which is that by positioning Toby as a directorial surrogate and having him stare down an image of heavy industry, Wheatley is acknowledging his own shifting position as a filmmaker. High-Rise had its world premiere in the newly devised “Platform” section of the Toronto International Film Festival alongside titles that could by and large be described as “art cinema”(and it lost the inaugural Platform jury prize to the unheralded Canadian documentary Hurt, directed by Alan Zweig) but its ultimate reckoning has been (and will continue to be) at the global box office. At £5 million pounds, High-Rise is Wheatley's most expensive production to date, and the first not produced under the Rook Films banner (the financing was organized by Jeremy Thomas’ Recorded Picture Company). High Rise’s festival debut and release in the UK was accompanied by a massive advertising push by distributor Studio Canal, framing the film as an instant cult classic (the poster design evoked A Clockwork Orange). The mixed reviews only fueled the notion of a paradigm-shifting work, even as the relatively small cohort already familiar with Wheatley’s earlier films worried in the other direction, about a vision potentially compromised by corporate interests. In March of 2016, Wheatley and his collaborators embarked on a massive press tour involving screenings in dozens of cities in the UK, as well as stops in New York and Vancouver. In an image as dislocatingly strange as anything in his films, Wheatley sat for an interview (with Hiddleston, Miller and Evans) on NBC's strenuously innocuous Today Show before the film's American premiere at the Tribeca Film Festival. Wheatley's newest feature (too new for this book), the Boston-set action thriller Free Fire, boasts a producer credit by Martin Scorsese and a cast that includes a recent Oscar winner (Brie Larson) alongside old hand Michael Smiley, and has as of this writing been picked up by the powerful American distributor A24. It has also been reported that Wheatley is planning a remake of George Henri-Clouzot’s 1953 classic thriller The Wages of Fear, but with the story transposed to Africa and female leads – a switch appropriate in a post- Mad Max: Fury Road (2015) zeitgeist). It would seem, then, that the question that was asked in 2010– “Is this the future of British cinema”? – has changed in short order. Wheatley is a vanguard figure, and the issue now, at least for some, is whether High-Rise and its cast of international stars and noisy marketing blitz represent the “selling out” of a director whose early work positioned him as an outsider – with all the frustrations and freedoms that go along with being untethered. That so many critics reacted ambivalently to High-Rise’s manic energy is at once unsurprising and suggestive of what happens when an idiosyncratic artist’s work appears on a (relatively) larger stage, and could be taken as a heathy sign. But where Wheatley and Jump seem bemused by Laing's desire to fit in to the apartment block, their film’s very existence could be seen as a form of artistic social climbing, away from the defiantly independent ethos of Down Terrace (a drama set in a tiny house) and towards the potential compromises of larger financing structures, which have ensnared any number other hard-driving, independently oriented tyros, and which find their allegorical match in the vertically integrated expanse of the high-rise. With this in mind, it may be telling that the very final shot of High-Rise is not of Toby, or the building, but rather a bubble floating up into the sky. Its appearance could be an S.O.S. of sorts -- a symbol of a fragile economic reality about to pop. Or, coming as it does right in front of Wheatley's directorial credit – which is placed onscreen for the same time as Jump's “written by” credit, a definitive indicator of artistic equivalency – it could indicate that the filmmakers aren't taking their film and its distress-call implications all that seriously. (Perhaps they're bursting their own bubble). Or, it might finally be nothing more or less than an ephemeral cousin to that rainbow in Kill List -- another rich, sinister enigma hovering implacably above the confusion and carnage below.
