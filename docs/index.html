2026-09-23 Never After Dark (2026) davidehrlich secondary 3.0 no A slow-burn ghost story that seems to be playing into convention even as it sneakily plants the seeds to turn the genre on its head, Dave Boyle’s clever and involving “Never After Dark” starts with a rather familiar premise: A medium is hired to expunge a dark spirit from a big house in the middle of nowhere. In this case, the “middle of nowhere” is a massive house in the forests of Hokkaido (a real property in Kiyosato that feels like it was designed as a fan tribute to the Overlook Hotel), and the medium is a jaded young thirtysomething who’s more afraid of the living than she is of the dead. But, in the first of the movie’s curious wrinkles, there’s an unusual reason why Airi — played by “Shōgun” highlight Moeka Hoshi, who delivers a fun and forceful performance that’s always teetering right on the edge of control — is so comfortable with the deceased: She’s intimately haunted by a (sweet and encouraging) ghost of her own, that of her teenaged big sister, Miku (Kurumi Inagaki), who’s still and forever the same age that she was when she died. That introductory detail proves to be an important clue as to how this movie will play with time, as Boyle’s spare and patient script gradually begins to conflate the thin line between this world and the next with the line between the present and the past — to blur one of those boundaries is also to blur the other. That confusion is somewhat implicit in all ghost stories, but “Never After Dark” seizes upon it in fascinatingly direct ways as Airi struggles to exorcize her latest target, and its “been there, done that” things going bump in the night first half gives way to a second one in which the film’s ronin ghostbuster is forced to mix things up and banish a living person to the other side of the veil instead of banishing a dead person’s ghost from this one. Now you might be thinking: “Isn’t ‘banish a living person to the other side of the veil’ just medium-speak for committing murder?” And… well, yes. But it’s a testament to the detail of Boyle’s film — which is modestly scaled but immersively self-contained — that “Never After Dark” is able to redefine the rules of this mortal coil on its own terms. That starts with the mechanics of Airi’s practice, which are as familiar in function as they are novel in form, and, typical of the “Man from Reno” filmmaker’s work, blend Eastern and Western tropes into the stuff of a singular genre exercise. ~this review continues on IndieWire~
