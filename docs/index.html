2026-10-06 Artificial (2026) davidehrlich secondary 3.0 no In case anyone at the world premiere of Luca Guadagnino’s “Artificial” wasn’t already thinking of the OpenAI biopic as an unofficial sequel to “The Social Network,” the screening was unexpectedly preceded by a musical performance from Damon Albarn, in which the Blur frontman and occasional movie composer was joined onstage by a youth choir for a rendition of an original dirge he wrote for the film about how we’re all singing our way straight into hell. Sixteen years ago, a bunch of angelic little kids belting out “Creep” was the perfect introduction to a sneering chamber drama about the punks whose self-hatred turned the 21st century against itself. Today, now that Silicon Valley’s nerd Prometheis have graduated from getting revenge on Rooney Mara to threatening the future of the human race, the kids are back for a more existential lament. They haven’t grown up, and neither have we. Once upon a time, a snively Harvard undergrad rewired the entire world only to discover that he couldn’t escape the empty pit of his own soul. Less than two decades after that, another Peter Thiel-funded sociopath bet our species on transcending the limits of humanity, only to prove that we can’t help but encode it into everything we create. It’s the same old story all over again, albeit presented here at the scale that computer scientist Ilya Sutskever (“Anora” breakout Yura Borisov) spent most of his career desperate for the data to achieve. History repeats itself, first as tragedy, second as farce — or in this case, as an uncanny sort of San Francisco camp. To be honest, I struggled to imagine why a sensualist like the director of “Challengers” and “Call Me by Your Name” would be interested in something as cold and algorithmic as the story of OpenAI. Andrew Garfield, who plays OpenAI CEO Sam Altman as a machiavellian queen bee with a penchant for upspeak and a frighteningly blasé attitude toward replacing mankind with computers (imagine an apocalyptic gay cross between Regina George and Agent Smith), has suggested that Guadagnino was exploring “how far we can take our obsession with not being human before we return to the body,” but this movie never strays far enough from the body to require a return. Garfield’s bulging arms. Borasov’s patchy hair, already receding into lines of code when the movie first introduces Sutskever at the age of 17. Monica Barbaro’s oaken brown eyes, which lend OpenAI chief technology officer Mira Murati the patina of a moral code. Even the pancreas of a character’s unseen wife, the sickness in which AIG promises to double back and cure if only everyone keeps hurtling forward. “Artificial” focuses its attention on these details as if it were training a neural network to create associations between them. To “yes, and” Garfield’s supposition, perhaps it would be more accurate to say that Guadagnino is exploring how difficult it is to abandon the body in the first place — and not just the body, but the intangible essence that it contains. We couldn’t leave it behind if we tried, and this, in his own way, is Guadagnino trying just as hard to do that as Sam Altman ever did. ~this review continues on IndieWire~
