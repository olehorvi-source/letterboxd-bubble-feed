2026-11-18 Paper Tiger (2026) thejoshl primary 4.0 no This review may contain spoilers. James Gray spent much of his early career processing his feelings about his Russian-Jewish immigrant upbringing in Queens via the classically controlled New Hollywood formal trappings of a Coppola family crime tragedy—taking these intimate, personal autobiographical experiences and rendering them as handsomely crafted crime-thrillers and operatic melodrama. Whether it was working through his mother’s terminal illness in Little Odessa , his father’s involvement in an MTA corruption scandal in The Yards , or the traumatic trickle down effect these events had on he and his brother during the pressurized backdrop of a vast and scary, organized-crime-ridden 80s NYC in all of the above + We Own the Night , where Gray depicts it as a form of generational/emotional pollution that made their family and home feel unsafe. After a brief foray into bigger worlds and budgets (space in Ad Astra , the Amazon in Lost City of Z ), Paper Tiger sees Gray return back to the small-scale borough milieu where his career started but with a matured eye and an even more fully formed stylistic rigor. He even brought back cinematographer Joaquín Baca-Asay to use many of the same vintage lights and lenses to get that soft, grainy, textured, warm 70s film glow he loves to play with. In some ways it’s comforting to see a master go back to the toolkit they know so well and play The Hits: doorframes, golden hues, police corruption, slow zooms, construction sites, Russian mobsters, secret Bright Beach meetings, tall grass, window reflections, etc. But I’m happy to report that those surface-level pleasures are not the entirety of what’s going on here. This doesn’t play like a career course correction as much as a recurring haunting that’s been gnawing at him, an attempt to replay some of these events less from the perspective of angry wallowing kid he once was, and more from the weary, worried parent seeking forgiveness he now is. So, though this certainly sees Gray once again densely layering in his fascination with this NYC history (specifically the crossover between Eastern European/Jewish assimilation, organized crime and capitalist American Dream aspirations) into unbearably tense slow-burn crime thrills and tragic family portraiture. Rather than his usual perspective of two brothers caught in a compromising/corrupting system reckoning with or resisting their domineering father figure, this time the brothers (Teller’s brainy engineer Irwin, Driver’s street-smart former cop-businessman Gary, both excellent) are the elder father/uncle figures to a younger set of teenage sons. And it's their mistakes, namely a misjudgment of a calculated risk on who to get into business with and a subsequent failure to shield their loved ones from the dangers lurking just outside their banal middle-class windows, that comes back to existentially terrify and scar them. It’s a subtly new vantage point for him, that offers up different possibilities of where and how to find the forms of neurosis, anguish, moral ambiguity, and painful subjectivity he’s attracted to in his stories. ScarJo, for example, as Teller’s wife Hester is the type of character who might get sidelined in a drama like this in favor of the macho brotherly melodrama, but I was surprised to find her given some pretty intense and complicated interludes of her own in what becomes something of a triptych structure. The way this depicts her blurry and then sudden realization of what’s happening to her brain and her decision to keep it an unspoken secret among the house’s other extreme worries is really well done, especially that early car crash with the sun in her eyes. The kind of detail a kid in the car when it happens will remember. But even more effective than that sequence, is a mid-film mob goon home invasion which despite being rather low-key in terms of what physically, logistically takes place is among the most menacing things Gray has ever filmed. Teller’s wide eyes and frozen silhouetted shadow on the wall (making his kids BB gun look like a bigger protective threat than it is, it is called Paper Tiger after all!), the polaroid flash that opens the scene followed by the discovery of what they contain… The fact that he never sees the intruders but hears and senses them like this looming, overwhelming force that’s threatening what should be a safe, civil space. It’s a very powerful and controlled way of depicting someone realizing their loss of control, their powerlessness, their anxiety about and inability to fully know what lies beyond their vision/world. A similar idea expressed via the entire finale with Driver, whose confident tough-guy shell he wears in front of his brother like the many nice suits he rocks eventually exposes a moving, raw-nerve desperation. (Paper tigers everywhere!) The line of work he’s chosen requiring commitment, sacrifice and absolution, and of course resulting in an unsaid final goodbye (through a doorframe with different color temperatures!) and your Classic Gray Tall Grass Shootout that’s basically the one from We Own the Night if it took some of its timing and rhythm cues (and airplane jet engine sound design) from Heat . Just pure cinema excellence by a master who knows which greats to steal from, including himself. I’m all for him continuing to do this final shot over and over again until he’s fully satisfied with it. The poetry of the movie opening with Teller fitting storm windows to the house from the outside looking in, to it closing with him gazing out as his kids go out into the world, despite their newfound knowledge of what’s out there; the Mercedes in the reflection, the dramatic irony of what we know but he doesn’t about his brother/wife… It’s so intricately loaded with meaning and feeling! This is why he’s one of the best, man. “I took risks… and we already had everything.”
