His career has greatly benefited as a result of his decision to relocate his family from their home in Spain to the minimalist glass mansion he’s designed for them in the heart of a gentrified London neighborhood, but the more accomplished he gets, the more isolated he appears to become. His marriage to literary executive Sofia (a sorely under-written role played by Bardem’s real-life wife Penélope Cruz) has withered in the growing shadow of his celebrity profile. He can barely communicate with his phone-addicted teenage daughter, he doesn’t have any time to play with the younger one who’s nearing the end of her cutest years, and his every waking thought is consumed by the window-sized hole that his next-door neighbor (Stephen Graham) has just hammered into the wall that separates their properties. After “The Father” (extraordinary) and “The Son” (not), Zellner has returned with a characteristically bleak vision of the holy ghost; in this case, the invisible specter of fear that causes so many of the modern world’s richest and/or most accomplished people to isolate themselves from the rest of humanity in response to their good fortune. 2026-09-08 Fires on the Plain (2014) thejoshl primary 4.0 no The first in what has become Shinya Tsukamoto's Late Style anti-war era, and a re-adaptation of the same semi-autobiographical WWII novel by Ōoka Shōhei that was also the source of Kon Ichikawa's 1959 film about a starving, tuberculosis-stricken Japanese private who during the collapse and retreat of the Imperial Army wanders the Philippine jungles witnessing various degrading and madness-induced survivalist horrors (including, eventually, cannibalism). Tsukamoto's background in ugly, bad-taste, psychologically perverse body-horror, and often assuming the delirious and tortured lead roles himself makes his attraction to this disgusting material and character pretty obvious. But what's really fascinating (and what has either worked for you or not about his recent output) is how thoroughly he's embraced cheap, harsh-looking digital as a modern replacement for the grainy, experimental lo-fi film look of his early cyberpunk work. Lots of natural over-exposed lighting and handheld camcorder shooting techniques that land somewhere between too-real looking roughness and artificial cosplay. It's not far removed from what Mann decided to do with digital cameras when he made the transition only instead of an impressionistic poetry Tsukamoto is in pursuit of the ugly and uncanny. Every dirty, rotting, unpleasant texture in unbearable close-up, every burst of violence this sudden disorienting POV lurch like it's happening right in front of you. When a head explodes via grenade/gunfire, or a dog gets bayonetted, or a pile of corpses is stumbled upon, blood water is swam through, it's like this digital docudrama hyper-reality that Tsukamoto then takes to an even further extreme with slow-motion, dissolves and some of the most ridiculous gore and blood squibs you'll see outside of the an 80s splatter movie. A mid-film night-time massacre in particular features as many intestine leaking, flying limbs, face peeling and (stomped on) brain matter and foggy blue lighting to rival Fulci. And as it goes on Tsukamoto's face gets dirtier, his eyes more hollowed out, his flesh more maggot-infected (and eaten!), his sadistically miserable "we have sights to show you" approach to butchering wartime horror giving way to a seemingly unending waking death dream. A sense of gut-wrenching terror at the idea that this book was written 60 years ago and "it's happening again!"
