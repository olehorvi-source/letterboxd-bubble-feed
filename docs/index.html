2026-10-11 Wildwood (2026) davidehrlich secondary 3.0 no On the one hand, Travis Knight’s “Wildwood” is an epic, eye-popping, and almost unfathomably well-crafted celebration of everything that makes Laika special — of everything that makes the Oregon-based stop-motion studio a singular force for good in a film industry where most companies are less interested in savoring the magic of animation than they are in seeing how many shortcuts they can take without breaking its spell. On the other hand, the remarkable time and care that went into “Wildwood” doesn’t prevent it from being the least enchanting of Laika’s films, even if its flaws help to reinforce the power of a story about how magic can persist in spite of itself. The longest and most ambitious movie of its kind ever made, this sprawling fantasy saga about the overlap between reality and imagination — about a teenage girl in the Pacific Northwest who ventures into an enchanted kingdom to rescue her toddler little brother after he’s abducted there by a murder of crows — pushes the form to new extremes at a time when the rest of the business is hoping that audiences will learn to settle for less. It’s a mad feat of muchness. Laika 3D-printed 142,000 different face models, hand-molded 728 individual trees, and used more than 715 miles of brass wire across 1,155 days of production. They created a “Braveheart”-style battle one frame at a time, built a perfect miniature replica of Powell’s City of Books to bring the movie’s version of Portland to life (complete with a Drag Queen Story Hour sign), and even designed tiny little Decemberists posters to adorn the heroine’s living room; she seems to be more of a fan of their post-“The Crane Wife” output, but I’m trying my best not to hold that against her. That degree of loving attention comes at a massive price, of course (with one negligible exception, each of Laika’s films has cost more and grossed less than the one before it), but studio owner and Nike co-founder Phil Knight can certainly afford it, and it doesn’t hurt that his son is running the shop. Not all Hollywood nepo babies! In lieu of any prolonged fawning over the pursuits of a billionaire heir, let’s just say the Skydance merger would probably seem a bit less cataclysmic if David Ellison were capable of directing something as artful and transcendent as “Kubo and the Two Strings” (or even something as competent and colorful as last summer’s live-action “Masters of the Universe,” for that matter). Which is all to say that “Wildwood” epitomizes why Laika should be treated like a national treasure: It’s stunning, it’s sincere, it’s possessed by a combination of American moxie and a Ghibli-esque willingness to push young audiences out of their comfort zones, and it’s seemingly at peace with losing enough money to sink any other animation studio on the planet. It is, in so many respects, the ultimate testament to what makes Laika unique. It’s also, in so many others, the first of their movies that doesn’t feel as special as it looks (and sometimes doesn’t look as special as it should feel). Appropriately enough for a project that suggests Laika’s care may not be enough to preserve its magic, “Wildwood” is a story about losing faith in the wonder of the world. And appropriately enough for a project that still has so much to offer in spite of its flaws, “Wildwood” is also a story about how magic can persist in our lives even after we stop believing in it — even after its child-like purity gives way to something mottled and imperfect. ~this review continues on IndieWire~
