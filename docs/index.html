2026-09-23 Never After Dark (2026) davidehrlich secondary 3.0 no A slow-burn ghost story that seems to be playing into convention even as it sneakily plants the seeds to turn the genre on its head, Dave Boyle’s clever and involving “Never After Dark” starts with a rather familiar premise: A medium is hired to expunge a dark spirit from a big house in the middle of nowhere. In this case, the “middle of nowhere” is a massive house in the forests of Hokkaido (a real property in Kiyosato that feels like it was designed as a fan tribute to the Overlook Hotel), and the medium is a jaded young thirtysomething who’s more afraid of the living than she is of the dead. But, in the first of the movie’s curious wrinkles, there’s an unusual reason why Airi — played by “Shōgun” highlight Moeka Hoshi, who delivers a fun and forceful performance that’s always teetering right on the edge of control — is so comfortable with the deceased: She’s intimately haunted by a (sweet and encouraging) ghost of her own, that of her teenaged big sister, Miku (Kurumi Inagaki), who’s still and forever the same age that she was when she died. That introductory detail proves to be an important clue as to how this movie will play with time, as Boyle’s spare and patient script gradually begins to conflate the thin line between this world and the next with the line between the present and the past — to blur one of those boundaries is also to blur the other. That confusion is somewhat implicit in all ghost stories, but “Never After Dark” seizes upon it in fascinatingly direct ways as Airi struggles to exorcize her latest target, and its “been there, done that” things going bump in the night first half gives way to a second one in which the film’s ronin ghostbuster is forced to mix things up and banish a living person to the other side of the veil instead of banishing a dead person’s ghost from this one. Now you might be thinking: “Isn’t ‘banish a living person to the other side of the veil’ just medium-speak for committing murder?” And… well, yes. But it’s a testament to the detail of Boyle’s film — which is modestly scaled but immersively self-contained — that “Never After Dark” is able to redefine the rules of this mortal coil on its own terms. That starts with the mechanics of Airi’s practice, which are as familiar in function as they are novel in form, and, typical of the “Man from Reno” filmmaker’s work, blend Eastern and Western tropes into the stuff of a singular genre exercise. ~this review continues on IndieWire~

Recruiting non-professional actors on both sides of this post-McCarthy “unamerican” battle royale to stage a (barely) fictional near-future where Nixon has declared emergency martial law against all left-wing/counter-cultural/anti-war dissidents and activists and thrown them into the harsh conditions of the Mojave Desert to be brutally hunted for sport by national guardsman and cops. Adopting an observational, handheld vérité film crew style that allows for grounded and spontaneous realism even when he is (again, barely) exaggerating the speculative sci-fi dystopia circumstances; enhancing the absurd, satirical bleakness and viciousness with a legitimately nightmarish and upsetting snuff newsreel psychodrama reality. Locating gut-punches in the way it will sharply crosscut between the activists in heated debate/on literal trial for their righteously “violent” words against the fascist politicians running the country and the gruesomely literal state violence enacted on them as a form of punitive deterrent. 2026-09-22 Forgotten Island (2026) davidehrlich secondary 3.0 no There is a lot going on in Joel Crawford and Januel Mercado’s “Forgotten Island,” a heartfelt but exhaustingly hyper-frenetic Dreamworks Animation comedy that remixes “Superbad,” “Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind,” and “The Hangover” into a rave-like children’s adventure steeped in Filipino folklore. It’s a touching story of memory and friendship (one that sees the “How to Train Your Dragon” and “Kung Fu Panda” production studio doubling down on the raw emotionality that worked so well for “The Wild Robot”), and also a manic cartoon that goes out of its way to make a joke about Will Smith slapping Chris Rock at the Oscars (because Dreamworks is still the house that “Shrek” built, at the end of the day). It’s a heavily researched but kid-friendly glimpse into a culture that Hollywood has never drawn to life before, and also an anime-inspired piece of cultural syncretism that repeatedly incorporates actual footage from the Arnold Schwarzenegger classic “Predator.” Did I mention that Dave Franco voices a cool pubescent weredog — a combination of words that no human has ever typed before — who uncontrollably transforms into a tiny chihuahua whenever he gets too close to a stick? Like I said, it’s a lot. Sometimes — frequently — too much. But it’s hard to complain about “too much” at a time when most of the animated movies this country pumps out aren’t nearly enough. While “Forgotten Island” is relentless, distractable, and overstuffed to a degree that waters down the simple power of its message (and makes it difficult to understand the basic principles of its plot), none of its lovingly sourced ingredients ever lose the basic essence of their flavor. ~this review continues on IndieWire~
