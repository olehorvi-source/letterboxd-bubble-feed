2026-11-11 How to Rob a Bank (2026) davidehrlich secondary 2.5 no The major Hollywood studios tend to shy away from the subject of class warfare because they know they can’t pull them off with a straight face. But young people in this country are increasingly animated by radical leftist ideas like “earning a livable wage” and “being able to buy a home before they die,” and at a certain point the movies will have to meet them where they live. Perhaps that point is now. How else to explain why Jeff Bezos would fund a Robin Hood-esque heist thriller that not only encourages its target demographic to commit armed robberies, but also kinda sorta teaches them how? (No one is going to confuse a David Leitch movie for “The Anarchist Cookbook.”) At a time when internet activists like Hasan Piker have come to occupy more space in the public imagination than Marvel superheroes, selling the masses on fanciful stories about the downward redistribution of wealth might be the billionaire class’ best protection against the threat of it actually happening in real life. It’s a lot more fun than paying a proportional tax rate, at least. Of course, the poppy and frenetic “How to Rob a Bank” wouldn’t dare to take aim at massive corporations that widen the wealth gap by destroying small businesses, normalizing exploitative labor practices, and accelerating worldwide environmental collapse. This is very specifically a film about how banks are bad and should be fucked with, an opinion that the founder of Amazon might actually share with the average ticket-buyer. And it’s not like the director of “Bullet Train” suddenly made a serious call to action that’s poised to inspire a wave of copycat robberies. Shot with all the propulsiveness of “Trainspotting” but almost none of its unbridled lust for life, Leitch’s YouTube-era fairy tale is no more dangerous to America’s power structures than “Verity” or “Project Hail Mary” (to name some of its studio’s other recent titles). Compared to the lawlessness of “Grand Theft Auto VI,” it might as well be “Nintendo Switch Sports Resort.” Still, its cast is hot, its heart is in the right place, and while the Amazon Punk of it all has a tendency to make its fantasy seem more like a sale, there’s something indivisibly real and relatable to the premise of a heist movie whose characters feel like they have to steal the most basic principles of the life they were promised. Ryan (Nicolas Hoult, channeling a very effective smidgeon of the destructive intensity that made him so terrifying in “The Order”) isn’t trying to get rich or spark a revolution, he and his accomplices just want a fair shot at the same thing that previous generations have been afforded as part of the American contract: The chance to support his family, and maybe a fair shot at revenge against the people and/or system who denied him that opportunity. In most respects, that shot has never been more unfair than it is now, but the internet — which has become the most powerful system for socioeconomic oppression this side of sharecropping — has turned attention into a currency more valuable than cash, and young people are still inventing new ways to harness those riches for themselves. For Ryan, that process starts with stealing money the old-fashioned way, as he and his four-person crew film themselves knocking off every bank in Pittsburgh (their identities protected by giant animal masks). But the cash seems to be less important than the content that it buys them, as the gang’s real passion is reserved for the splashy, hi-octane YouTube tutorials that Hugo (an upbeat Rhenzy Feliz) cuts together and posts online after each job. “You know what’s more powerful than a gun?,” Ryan says. “An audience.” That’s us! We rule. ~this review continues on IndieWire~

2026-11-11 Born to Kill (1947) thejoshl primary 3.0 no A terrifically thorny, subversive premise realized by two very good actors: Claire Trevor as the tortured femme fatale who tries to performatively live like an innocent socialite but is instinctually drawn to strength and danger, and Lawrence Tierney (50 years before Reservoir Dogs !) as the temperamental, sociopathic murderer who takes and does whatever he wants. The magnetic pull these two have is both scary and liberating because unlike the wealthy people around them both are aware of how frank and brutal one needs to be to socially/financially climb in this rotten world. Unfortunately, as much as I see why this was shocking and salacious for the time, I think Robert Wise plays it all a little too tame after the opening kitchen double-murder, which is so sudden and casual for these characters that I expected it to turn into more a perverse crime spree story whereas instead it mostly settles into a mansion melodrama. The morbid subject matter and psychology gives it some power for sure (I especially like Trevor’s sudden venomous turns when she lets her dark insides out: “Perhaps you don't realize it's painful being killed—a piece of metal sliding into your body, finding its way into your heart - or a bullet tearing through your skin, crashing into a bone”) , but not as much as I thought it would based on how good the two performances are. It’s perfectly competent and handsome, and its doomed finale is quite good, but there’s just honestly not as many memorably eerie images or suspense setpieces as this set-up could’ve been milked for imo. Esther Howard and Elisha Cook Jr. fill out the supporting cast nicely as the eccentric drunk boarding house proprietor and sympathetic friend who tries to help them get away with their crime though. 2026-11-11 Coyote vs. Acme (2026) thejoshl primary 4.0 no I kinda wish more of the animation was hand-drawn and the live-action had less of that bright TV ad sheen to it, but otherwise this is a total front-to-back riot. Chalk full of clever satirical ideas and manic gags-within-gags visual details, and gets pretty much everything right that I've loved about my sadistic slapstick little buddies since watching endless Teletoon Retro reruns. I didn't realize at the time those cartoons were teaching me the language of the great silent era comedians, so it wasn't until I started watching Keaton, Chaplin, Lloyd, etc (and really thinking about them), that it occurred to me what their artistry really was. That what I'd really been laughing at all this time was in some ways real existential pain and suffering, and that through that laughter I was also bonding and feeling for these clowns as profoundly as I might a really well-done melodrama. So it was a really beautiful surprise that that cathartic realization I had later in life was quite literally the subject of this movie. What if Sisyphus and the boulder were two lonely souls with a mutually-agreed-upon, symbiotic arrangement, and ultimately kind of loved each other? That's a purity we can't let any corporation poison or exploit. My love of Who Framed Roger Rabbit? as a dorky kid resulted in me looking up and watching a lot of noirs/conspiracy-thrillers the movie was satirizing, so I got a little teary-eyed thinking about the kid who will do the same for this and corporate legal dramas—end up seeing The Insider or Michael Clayton or something. Anyway, John Cena talking in Tweety-speak nearly had me fall out of my seat, as did the Pepé Le Pew cameo.
