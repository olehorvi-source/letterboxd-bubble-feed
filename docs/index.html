That man’s name was Bowfinger. The comedy that Frank Oz made about him was also called “Bowfinger.” And the joke — the joke that teed up every other joke — was that “Chubby Rain” was obviously a very, very stupid idea for a movie. Even a movie that only cost $2,184. Netflix’s “The Last House” cost a lot more than that, but god damn is its rain chubby. A thriller so dumb that — in bygone smarter times — a central aspect of its premise was used as a punchline, this late summer washout updates Bowfinger’s masterpiece into an asinine COVID parable where the rain gets so fat that people can no longer go outside. Literally. What first appears to be a run-of-the-mill Seattle rainstorm is soon revealed to be something more nefarious, as the apparently sentient precipitation seals everyone inside of their homes. Doors are welded shut. Windows are somehow rendered unbreakable. Neighbors are forced to communicate with poster boards like in Zack Snyder’s “Dawn of the Dead.” It’s a good thing that unemployed engineer and hobbyist fisherman Jason (Wagner Moura, hopefully reeling in a decent paycheck) makes it back to his wife Ann (a similarly overqualified Greta Lee) and their two kids before the clouds roll in, otherwise he would have been left to fend for himself amidst the early morning runners and the increasingly feral local wildlife. 2026-08-06 Gordon's War (1973) thejoshl primary 4.0 no Sorta like if Point Blank and The French Connection were fused together into a cheap Blaxploitation crime-procedural about a crew of black Vietnam veterans (led by a terrifically stoic Paul Winfield) who come home and deploy their guerilla war tactics in service of vigilante violence: dismantling the vast network of drug dealers and pimps in Harlem from the ground up. Gets its logline setup and crew recruitment out of the way within about 15 minutes, and then essentially turns into a series of excitingly pragmatic (borderline documentary-esque at times) planning and execution sequences until it rolls credits: kidnapping a dealer and injecting him with his own product, staging a fake overdose to scare some kids straight, raiding and demolishing a blacklight drug den, pulling off an Ocean’s safe heist, eventually exploding into a rundown rusty warehouse red paint squib shootout and an explosive car vs. motorcycle chase through the streets of Harlem that looks as dangerous as anything Friedkin ever filmed. You don't really learn much about this guys except what their capable of on the job but I kind of liked that about the film. Honestly everything about the way this is shot (shoutout gritty 70s crime film goat Victor J. Kemper), scored (Angelo Badalamenti was in a funk band??), and assembled for maximum momentum is pretty impressive considering I hadn’t heard of this before. There’s a lot of letting audio from conversations that’s allowed to run underneath footage of the action the voices are building to already under way that’s really effective, and there’s a triple attempted hit sequence against the crew that’s crosscut together so they all climax at the same time (one successful, the other two not) that is just as good as this genre gets. And if that wasn’t enough, this was directed by the great actor and activist turned director Ossie Davis (who read the eulogy at Malcolm X’s funeral), so of course he suggests even after the crooks are cathartically dispatched the work is far from over, and he continues even further up the chain to the white boardrooms all this ground-level decay is originating with. Insanely underseen and underrated imo. Full discussion coming soon on the podcast .

2026-08-06 Dead Presidents (1995) thejoshl primary 4.0 no Always assumed, partially based on the striking poster image of a lone gunman in The Crow make-up, that this was somewhere in line with the 90s heist movie craze ( Point Break , Set It Off , etc); so was pleasantly surprised that this had so much Goodfellas and Bullet in the Head in it too. The Hughes Brothers getting the upgraded budget after Menace II Society and deciding to just throw as many different forms of genre pastiche at you as they can—coming-of-age melodrama, Vietnam war film, Blaxploitation neo-noir, gangster crime drama, and (eventually) heist thriller—that it feels like a bit of a greatest hits epic at times. Great cast (Keith David and Bokeem Woodbine MVPs), great soundtrack (so much Isaac Hayes!), and when all the disgusting slow-motion violence hits (almost American Heroic Bloodshed-esque at times), it’s with a truly tasteless level of unpleasant gore I really wasn’t expecting. But despite some of the Blaxploitation shorthand when it comes to ensemble character archetypes it indulges it’s not just a hollow music video cinephile surface you might be tempted to accuse it of. There is a clear passionate anger and anguish that fuels their mission here to put a subversively black American experience into these Hollywood modes of filmmaking. Taking harrowing accounts from Bloods: An Oral History of the Vietnam War by Black Veterans and court details from the 1981 Brink's robbery by members of the Black Liberation Army and realizing this recurring tragedy of economic desperation and racist horror via the visceral effectiveness of genre storytelling. The war and heist setpieces themselves are so muscular and controlled, and yet the emotional and political pain they are a gruesome manifestation of is never not on your mind as they unfold.
