2026-08-11 Pecker (1998) brofromanother primary no During the Make the Case pre-show last night at the Paradise (thanks to Simon Ennis for selecting the movie) the programmers used a quote from Manohla Dargis to stand in for the widespread critical disparagement of Pecker: "John Waters," I'm paraphrasing here but basically this is verbatim, "still doesn't know how to make a movie." Dargis of course knows how to watch a movie: she's a really good critic and not the type to miss things that have been intentionally put on screen for her consideration. But the idea of Pecker as 1) being badly crafted 2) worse for that fact belies the point of the exercise. “If my pictures were better,” says Edward Furlong’s “Teenage Weegee” to the culture vulture apprising his wares, “they wouldn’t be as great;” Waters has said Pecker is not his surrogate here (that’d be the little girl addicted to sugary snacks and Cola to the point of needing to be medicated) but darned if he doesn’t seem to be ventriloquizing his art through him – such as it is. Pecker is not artful but it has something to say about art. A few things in fact: that it’s a study of beauty; that it’s rooted in love; that it’s easily commodified but also – Waters being a true optimist – also potentially rescued from commodification. It also says that the commodifiers themselves are not bad people, just a little out to lunch, and themselves worthy and valuable subjects of affectionate deconstruction; this is a movie where Cindy Sherman not only shows up but shows she’s in on the joke (she offers the little sugar addict a Valium). The bit where a homeless couple crashes an invite only dinner nods, however obloquely, towards Viridiana, and Waters – who’s spoken of Bunuel reverently in the same breath as Bergman and Hershell Gordon Lewis – had aged by 1998 into a similar, discreetly charming style of bourgeois critique. Not mean, not lacerating, not defiant, but also not necessarily wimpy either; the jokes here include a cluster of Baltimore-ian seniors chanting angrily to “bring back Bush!” (double entendre intended) and Pecker and his laundomat-Mussolini sweetie Shelley (Christina Ricci) reconciling by hooking up loudly in a voting booth – privacy in public; sex as civic duty. Now as to whether Waters knows how to make a movie: of course he does. He makes it his way, which means quickly, cheaply, but still with a sense of pacing, timing, and – crucial for a character-driven independent comedy – a knack for getting the most out of his actors, each according to their ability. Furlong is not as good an actor as, say, Tobey Maguire, who was originally offered the role, and possibly would have brought more depth and shading and technique. But his smiley blankness – he’s like a benign cipher – works for a kid who’s curious about everyone and everything except himself, and who in the end really wants to do right by his family and friends and his talent, which the movie believes is real (a sour, obvious version of Pecker would imply that his work was fraudulent; even the satirical parodies of art-world prose hyping his "humane Diane Arbus" portraits err on the side of credibility). Writing is part of filmmaking and Waters is smart enough to not contrive any truly heavy-handed melodrama as his hero’s celebrity ascends and implodes; it’s all light and sweet and we know it’ll be alright. Ricci, who’s a great actor, is funnier and cuter and more distinctive in Pecker than Buffalo 66 or Opposite of Sex or what have you; I love how zealously she guards her laundromat’s code of conduct, and how she kisses the ground when their charter bus returns from New York to Baltimore (here *she’s* playing John Waters, if only for a moment). I’d like to bring up one other review - not cited during the pre-show – which was Roger Ebert’s two-star pan. Pretty rich for a guy who moralized about Pink Flamingos back in 1975 to bemoan the tension between Waters’ anarchic past and his mellowed-out maturity; to complain that nothing shocking happens. But he’s just so wrong about the subplot involving the Virgin Mary statue, which he says “could have provided comic possibilities, but doesn’t.” It sure does provide comic possibilities – a Catholic granny who claims her weird puppet speaks for God but keeps moving her lips during the benedictions; it’s funny! – but it also provides another quote that, like, the one about not being good being a source of greatness, proposes a way of watching the film as a whole. “Full of grace, full of grace,” she intones, over and over. “Full of grace.” She’s correct. “Better if Mary had been an art critic,” snarked Ebert. Wrong. She should have been a film critic.

2026-08-11 Seven Samurai (1954) thejoshl primary 5.0 yes I mean, it’s Seven Samurai … right? Does anyone really need to hear that it is one of the most foundational and influential works of action-adventure filmmaking construction—featuring some of the most astonishing tracking shots, production design, deep focus telephoto lensing, geometric control of framing/blocking, atmospheric visual (and sonic) use of weather, kinetic and momentum-driven editing, music, etc? Or that despite the broader cultural/historical portraiture and archetypal character storytelling it is an expressively personal work by a man whose father was an army physed instructor (who came from a samurai family) and older brother was a silent film obsessive, who together sent him down the path of painting, calligraphy, swordsmanship and John Ford that all merged here? There’s a reason it’s one of the first films they’ll make you watch in film school, and that’s because it is the exactingly meditative, harrowing and poetic black-and-white anti-war art film it has been endlessly written about as, as well as an extraordinarily work of genre entertainment, without which so many of the greatest westerns and men-on-a-mission films wouldn’t exist in the fashion they do. It has both an unmatched skill for patience and endurance in its storytelling, allowing the natural passing of time and (often comically humanizing, richly overlapping) bond between the samurai and farmers to develop into a larger philosophical relationship between the individual and the collective; and also climaxes on what’s practically 60+ straight minutes of stark, brutal, fiery and in-the-muck controlled chaos sword/archery/cavalry battle setpieces driven by a post-WWII mournfulness at the heavy cost of rampant militarism even when technically “victorious”. Few movies about the apocalyptic path of killing and death or the pain and misery of survival contain as much Toshiro Mifune bare ass cheeks on the prowl as seen here. And despite how hammy and funny his performance can be at times, when his peasant-turned-wannabe-samurai breaks down in front of his new samurai pals, delivering a venomously self-loathing rant about how the villagers are only deceptive and immoral because they’ve been fashioned that way through the decades of suffering and tragedy warriors have senselessly and indifferently inflicted on them, or when he holds that rescued baby in front of the burning mill realizing “this baby is me!”, there’s an animalistic pain in his eyes and voice that move me like only the best works of art can. Various stray notes/details I love: Takashi Shimura’s introduction mysteriously shaving his head, before we fully realize his plan disguise himself as a monk to save a child + watching his hair slowly grows back over the film. All the bursts of slow-motion as the bodies fall after the tense build-up to sudden swing of the swords. That during the recruitment process (a great mini-movie in and of itself) they pick up one guy they admit isn’t very good but he’s a locker room glue guy they just like, and that like an ironic bad omen he dies first! All the procedural covering of the defensive tactics and training, and consulting of the map that gets us involved in the spatial layout long before the bandits come attacking from every angle. The expert swordsman’s disappearance into the night to single-handedly retrieve one of the bandits muskets and his glacial emerging from the morning fog a few hours later—a visually striking moment that sets up the young samurai’s confession of admiration of him (the only time he smiles!), Mifune’s jealous and selfish attempt to perform the same act of heroism that results in him abandoning his post (which he did before to resue a baby effectively!) and leading to their most devastating day of losses, and the swordsman's eventual unjust death at the cowardly hands of a musket wielder!! The incredible delivery and music swelling on “This is the nature of war: by protecting others you save yourselves, if you only think of yourself you only destroy yourself” right before the intermission hits. The brief but powerful inclusion of the pain suffered by these villagers before the samurai arrived: the shot of the elderly lady coming for revenge with her harvesting hoe, the kidnapped wife who hurls herself into the burning hut. The insane scale of the wide contemplative shots of harvesting, burning homes, hilltop burial mounds vs. the overwhelming collapsed close-ups of the frantically muddy, rainy deglamorized violence. Idk. What else do you want from me? tldr; It’s Seven Samurai !!!!! Full discussion coming soon on the podcast .
