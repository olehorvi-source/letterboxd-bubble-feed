2026-09-29 Digger (2026) davidehrlich secondary 2.5 no Alejandro González Iñárritu and Tom Cruise were made for each other, if no one else. One is a cinematic daredevil whose films are fueled by the tension between ego and mortality — a mad virtuoso obsessed with staging elaborate stunts that externalize his inner being into the stuff of outsized spectacle, and do so with a kamikaze effortfulness so extreme that even (or especially) their most grandiose work can make it seem like the person behind them is hiding from reality instead of rendering it larger than life. The other is Tom Cruise. By different means, toward different ends, both men have consistently risked their creative and/or actual lives as they’ve reckoned with the limits of what they can accomplish with a movie camera. Through projects like “Birdman,” “Bardo,” and the “Mission: Impossible” sequels, both men have dedicated the most recent stretch of their careers to blurring the line between the art and the artist. Not as a bid to elide some kind of moral reckoning (easy as it is to see Cruise’s ascension into “The Savior of Movies” as a calculated rebrand), but rather as an effort to explore — or transcend — the relationship between reality and the stories that people choose to tell about themselves. In that light, I suppose it was only a matter of time before Iñárritu and Cruise finally combined their powers/personas to make the ultimate movie about self-delusion. And so we arrive at “Digger,” a raw and modest little movie that has no ambition beyond poking some fun at the absurdity of its own existence. Just kidding. LOL. ROTFL, even. The third chapter in a loose trilogy of postmodern films examining the value of creativity at a time when reality has become stranger than fiction, “Digger” is a mega-budget “Dr. Strangelove” riff so detached from human truth that its most believable character is a one-eyed animatronic cat named Maggie — a VistaVision satire so high-concept that it would be more accurate to measure its running time in altitude than minutes. Like “Birdman” and “Bardo” before it, Iñárritu’s biggest swing (and most endearing folly) self-reflexively hinges upon the various ways that people make sense of living in a world that has abandoned its logic. An $125 million+ Brechtian funhouse that’s been constructed with the craft of a basilica and the obviousness of a bad “SNL” cold open, “Digger” uses its ostensible premise — a trillionaire oil tycoon who doesn’t give a shit about climate change triggers an ecological catastrophe with his name all over it — to plumb the myopia that’s defined the 21st century, and might also be required to survive it. To say that Iñárritu’s characteristically belabored script (co-written by Sabina Berman, Alexander Dinelaris, and Nicolás Giacobone) reinvents itself at the end of its first and second acts would be the kind of understatement that the “Babel” director has never been capable of, but it’s safe to say that “Digger” sustains a clear, meta-textual interest in its central idea: The world is deteriorating faster than our collective imagination can keep up. They say that comedy is tragedy plus time, and this is a film that fracks that equation for every molecule of hot air that it can — a film that laughs at our compulsion to narrativize the past at the same time as it awes at our failure to appreciate how fucked things have gotten in the present. ~this review continues on IndieWire~
