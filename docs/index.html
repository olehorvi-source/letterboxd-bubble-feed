2026-09-06 Stealing Harvard (2002) theolejake my_account 3.5 no Tubi fed me this after Slackers, which turned out to be a breath of fresh air when the catalyst of the movie is helping a 17-year-old niece afford college rather than mentally undressing her, as so many other early-aughts comedies probably would. Jason Lee opens the movie as the archetypal Gen Xer, ready to explain what life is really all about. Then Tom Green shows up as Duff, an “exterior designer” who runs a landscaping business called Landscape Escape and loves melting blocks of cheese in the microwave. Every Tom Green scene is a reminder that some people are simply born funny. Truly up there with the greats who seemed to have comedy running through their bones. A shame he basically only starred in three mainstream comedies. Leslie Mann manages to turn what could have been the stock “nagging girlfriend” role into someone much more emotionally available than you’d expect. And then there are the terrific oddball moments: Dennis Farina and Richard Jenkins bonding over their dead wives, with Farina in a wig and nightgown, or John C. McGinley sincerely agreeing that the booze IS a bad scene. Stealing Harvard has more to offer and is far funnier than many movies of the same ilk.

2026-09-06 Rocky (1976) thejoshl primary 5.0 yes "Do you believe that America is the land of opportunity?" “Beethoven was deaf. Helen Keller was blind. I think Rocky's got a good chance.” Happy 50th to the American (dream) underdog sports movie that even watered down by sequels and copycats can't shake the fact that it is just a straight-up excellent dirty 70s working-class character piece drama/romance. Always somehow forget that it takes sixty minutes for Rocky to even find out what the plot of his movie is, ninety before The Montage hits, and there's maybe ten of any actual boxing. Spending most of its runtime taking in all the blunt kitchen sink Philly location photography and the charming warmth/sad small-scale socio-economic detail of both Rocky and the lives surrounding him in his periphery. The more macho anger and regret at the lack of upward momentum and the lost time as expressed via Paulie and Mickey contrasted with Rocky's willingness to take the same beating and remain sensitive and kind (love the bit where he even working as loan shark muscle he refuses to break the dude's thumbs because "I figure he gets laid off, he can't make money), and pursue any even small opportunity like his gradual and adorable courtship of Adrian, which is lowkey one of the most beautiful screen romances! "She's got gaps, I got gaps, together we fill gaps!" When he shouts her out on TV!! Stallone not only writing this script but insisting on himself in the role of the blue-collar himbo who moonlights as an amateur "Italian Stallion" boxer (and miraculously gets a chance to go the distance in a title fight with Weathers' Muhammad Ali-inspired Apollo Creed) has got to be one of the all-time greatest called shots by a writer or actor. It gives the film a natural purity and belief in itself, the production informing the character, and his puppy dog eyes and deep but often soft (hesitating, slurring) voice is half the magic trick of selling that this bruised and beaten slab of meat has a heart and soul and dreams that far exceed his impoverished environment. That it's never too late for a "bum from the neighborhood" to find love, or for a loser to win. The spectacled pageantry of the final fight giving way to intimate meatbag brutality, and its class/racial-tension loaded American Cinderella story ending with a simple assertion of dignity and value + lovers wailing out for each other + those horns!! Just the best. Cuff & Link also on the Mount Rushmore of movie character pets imo. Full discussion on ep 168 of my podcast SLEAZOIDS .

2026-09-06 Making Marie Antoinette (2026) davidehrlich secondary no The term “nepo baby” had yet to be coined by the time that Sofia Coppola — prematurely defined by her punchline of a performance in daddy Francis’ “The Godfather Part III” — re-emerged in her late 20s as one of the most gifted filmmakers of her generation, but few celebrities in her position, before or since, have ever been so acutely responsive to how they’re seen in the public eye. Drawing from lived experience in a way that allowed her to interpolate the biases she’d faced as both a Hollywood scion and a young woman in the arts, Coppola’s first two features directly confronted the sort of privilege and presumption that had shadowed her since childhood. Each in their own ways, “The Virgin Suicides” and “Lost in Translation” vividly traced the interior lives of girls who weren’t permitted to have them, and they did so with a hyper-real sensitivity that allowed the movies to resonate with anyone who’s ever felt observed but unseen, as if just out of frame in every picture that had ever been taken of them. Of course, success can — and often should — breed a special kind of resentment for nepo babies who dare to make a name for themselves, and so it was no surprise that people only sharpened their knives from “dinner” to “butcher” after Coppola won the Oscar for Best Original Screenplay in 2004. By the same token, it was, perhaps, even less of a surprise that Coppola’s response to such bitterness was to make a movie humanizing Marie Antoinette, the one-time Queen of France and eternal figurehead of unearned entitlement (“I know I will be able to express how a girl experiences the grandeur of a palace,” Coppola wrote to Antoinette biographer Antonia Fraser, whose book she hoped to use as source material). Least surprising of all? That a sizable percentage of critics and audiences were rather ungenerous towards the finished product, a $40 million costume drama more interested in the rhythms, customs, and furnishings of Antoinette’s gilded cage than it is in the anger her reign helped to foment beyond the palace walls. Never mind that Antoinette was a brilliantly natural subject for a filmmaker so compelled by the negative space between women and the worlds they inhabit, or that the movie’s panopticon-like vision of Versailles allowed Coppola to explore the relationship between power and personhood with peerless clarity, or that her gauzy direction invited the movie to love Marie Antoinette without ever letting her off the hook — or out of the guillotine, as it were (talk about having your cake and eating it too). Never mind that Coppola shrewdly offset her production’s unprecedented access to the actual palace grounds with a variety of inspired anachronisms (chief among them the single greatest soundtrack of the 21st century), and as a result created a sharply modern look at the unbearable lightness of being famous. Never mind!! Not that I was mad about that. Please don’t put in the newspaper that I got mad. Time has been kind to people who share my view that “Marie Antoinette” is a semi-understood masterpiece, and not only because critics like me have been increasingly emboldened to argue that case. (In 2022, I included it on my Sight & Sound ballot of the 10 greatest films ever made). Following years of murmured reconsideration, the film will be formally canonized into The Criterion Collection this November. And before that, MUBI will release — first in theaters, and then on its streaming platform — a feature-length documentary about the making of “Marie Antoinette” that is called, if you can imagine, “Making Marie Antoinette.” Despite what the 600-word preamble you just read might have led you to believe, that documentary is the true subject of this article. Sorry. But we all went on that long walk together for two reasons: 1.) I am physically and spiritually incapable of passing up any chance to get on my soapbox about the films of Sofia Coppola, whose formative impact on me would be impossible to overstate, and 2.) Because anyone reading this review of “Making Marie Antoinette” should know that I clicked my screener link for this movie like I was opening the fucking Ark of the Covenant. Footage of Rip Torn loosening up before a banquet scene, or of Jason Schwartzman trying to make Kirsten Dunst laugh before they get in the royal bed together… To some people, this might be the routine stuff of a vintage EPK, but to me it’s like watching DV video of Michelangelo painting the Sistine Chapel. ~this review continues on IndieWire~
