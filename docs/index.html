That the movie took place before Ishirō Honda’s original “Godzilla,” and 2.) That “people rise up from a postwar Japan that is at zero, and then Godzilla arrives, making the situation even worse and more tragic.” Continuing the story of its Oscar-winning predecessor while also doubling down on everything that made it the most acclaimed film in its franchise since the King of the Monsters first lumbered out of the Pacific Ocean in 1954, Yamazaki’s thunderously loud and frequently spectacular direct sequel has in turn been saddled with an even sillier title: “Godzilla Minus Zero.” To paraphrase the scene from “Sideways” where Paul Giamatti’s character reveals that his novel is called “The Day After Yesterday”: Isn’t that just… “Godzilla?” Yes and no. Sure, the title is absurd on its face, but — in keeping with Yamazaki’s logic — it also rather eloquently speaks to the premise of a film about a fragile people who are struggling to maintain (and build upon) the new equilibrium they’ve fought so hard to achieve in the years since their country became the epicenter of Earth’s nuclear era. Another mid-century melodrama with very occasional monster sightings, the more thematically coherent and emotionally affecting “Godzilla Minus Zero” expands on the naive grassroots nationalism of its predecessor by reframing that movie’s characters from victims to survivors, an emphasis that forces ex-kamikaze pilot Kōichi Shikishima (Ryunosuke Kamiki) and his friends to confront a series of impossible questions. 2026-09-27 Primetime (2026) thejoshl primary 4.0 no Lance Oppenheim has spent years experimenting with and honing a very specific, subjective and surreal form of "docu-fantasia" filmmaking that doesn't just observe or probe its subjects but tries to expressively access and convey their psychological reality. Taking documentary material and adopting a deliberately composed and arranged shooting/editing style more commonly deployed in fiction and, informed by what he learns about their environment and headspace, expanding on it with so much formal style that it can be consistently difficult to distinguish if what you’re watching is fly-on-the-wall documentation, collaboratively staged recreations or some kind of playfully blurred combination of the two in order to reach this hallucinatory "truth" about how they see and move through their worlds. So it was probably only a matter of time before material tempted him into the realm of the biopic, which he ultimately found via this distorted, hyper-stylized funhouse mirror of last year's Predators by David Osit. An unsettling documentary on the legacy of Chris Hansen and his controversial mid-aughts pedophile hunter sting operation entrapment reality TV show "To Catch a Predator", that didn't just reckon with the fundamental contradiction of if it was "even possible for something designed as entertainment to be a public service", but a deeper reckoning with the voyeuristic thrill of this ostensibly cathartic humiliation ritual, the lack of political progress or sociological/psychological explanation it offers to survivors, the nature of being complicit in something via consumption, and the use of technological tools to manipulate and dehumanize. Ideas Osit presents in about as careful and considerate yet still haunting/melancholy a fashion as I've seen from a documentary. I mention all this because having seen enough of the raw NBC footage excavated for that film (and having read the Esquire article “Tonight on Dateline This Man Will Die" this script was based on), it quickly became apparent just how little Oppenheim is aiming for anything resembling historical authenticity or accuracy than he is a nightmarish approximation, a luridly crafted and similarly self-aware form of shallow, crass sensationalism that Hansen might peddle in. It's a much weirder and trickier approach than expected, one that has a lot of opportunity to go extremely wrong if not calibrated correctly, and there are probably times where it maybe even tips too far, but I very much respect the decision to go for it even if it opens it up to valid critiques of smug hypocrisy. Which I could see rubbing some the wrong way due to the intensity of the subject matter but I've already seen the smart, sensitive take on this stuff (seriously, watch Predators ) and thus was very open to the stranger, trashier wavelength this opted for. Was genuinely in awe of the smeary, dreamy look that combines harsh aughts DV/hidden camera/tabloid media aesthetics with grainy and colorful kaleidoscopic showbiz/newsroom horror-procedural expressionism (the closest comparison would be like digital era Mann going back in time to reshoot Manhunter and The Insider ), and the performances that are so heightened and exaggerated they both play as repulsive comedy and confrontationally interrogate the meta quality at the heart of the original show which was trying to cage monstrous real-world behavior inside this staged, uncanny facsimile. Pattison is obviously electric as Hansen, not trying to mimic or impersonate as much as locate his own overly-rehearsed, elegant form of physical preying that lets the mask/skinsuit slip to reveal complicated layers of frantic, narcissistic and sociopathic "moral" fantasy driving his mission. Some genuinely chillingly alien tics going on here not seen since Gyllenhaal in Nightcrawler . But honestly Wever and especially Gisondo are almost as impressive, the former embodying the more causally cutthroat indifferent business side of this production, the latter the desperate, malleable and corruptible "performance" art of it all. I understand the position that what Oppenheim is getting at in terms of the actual "media satire" side of it all (I’ve seen Ace in the Hole and To Die For too pal) is ultimately a little thin and more than a little unfocused, but for me its deficiencies were overpowered by the sheer unsettling, obsessive mania it manages to embody with its style. Time travelling to a specific era of American tabloid-ratings culture/vigilante-bloodlust television spectacle not in any sort of grounded way, but a gleefully modern (and exploitative) way that triggers the same bad taste thrill part of your brain Oliver Stone did circa Natural Born Killers . There are many things I went into this movie expecting to see, and I can tell you that Chris Hansen psychedelically teleporting into an AOL chat room like he’s headed into the Stargate sequence from 2001 (which I guess makes the ancient pedophile demon the “Star Child”?) was not one of them. Which I don't think is legitimate psychoanalysis as much as an absurd attempt to make some sort of subjective sense out of a uniquely ugly, surreal time in our civilization where everyone was glued to their TVs and channel flipping between Lost , TCAP and the Iraq war. Either way, what a picture.
