2026-09-14 Love of Your Life (2026) davidehrlich secondary 3.5 no Rachel Morrison’s “Love of Your Life” is one of my favorite things, all too rare in today’s Hollywood: A gorgeously shot romantic drama about impossibly beautiful people that still manages to feel real enough where it matters. It’s the kind of smart, heartfelt, mid-weight entertainment that rewards you for surrendering to its logic and savoring how its light cuts through the darkness for 100 minutes — so naturally, since we live in hell, it’s only playing in theaters for a single week before it shuffles off to its forever home on Prime Video. I suppose we should be grateful that Morrison got to make it at all, as this is nothing if not a film that implores us to cherish what we still have left rather than rage at what’s been taken from us. It starts in a decidedly unromantic place: the emergency room of a Boston hospital, where joyless and withdrawn nurse Maya (Margaret Qualley) heals people as a way of attending to the unspecified family situation that scarred her as a child. Blasting Sharon Van Etten music on the subway and grinding out 18-hour shifts in her scrubs seems to be getting the job done, but happiness is a lot harder to deny when someone becomes determined to share it with you. In this case, that “someone” is Charlie (Gabriel Basso, far too charming for someone whose breakthrough role was playing J.D. Vance), a Mensa-smart goofball who smiles like a fool, dresses like a jock, and radiates an inner glow that shines a hole right through the lonerism that Maya has always maintained for her own protection. He was adopted at birth, and his worldview has been premised on accepting the love that he’s lucky enough to receive. Maya, by contrast, has a real “love isn’t worth the pain of losing it” vibe that has led her to stay as single and untethered as possible. At least until now. ~this review continues on IndieWire~
2026-09-14 The Life and Deaths of Wilson Shedd (2026) davidehrlich secondary 3.0 no Toward the beginning of Tim Blake Nelson’s “The Life and Deaths of Wilson Shedd,” a downhearted but defiantly humane prison drama that skips around in time as it maps an Oklahoma man’s journey from childhood poverty to his taxpayer-funded murder at the hands of the state, someone comments that people don’t get out of prison without a little help. There’s always a guard on the take, a civilian who manages to lend an assist from the outside, or — as the case may be — a creative writing teacher who develops a crush on one of her inmate students. That much is self-evident. Of ultimately greater interest to Nelson is the fact that people don’t get into prison without a little help either. Despite the infinite vastness of America’s carceral system, precious few of its convicted residents were born with some innate desire to kill. That’s not to say they’re innocent, of course (although far too many of them are), but simply to acknowledge the invisible weight of their circumstances. Nobody prefers a bruised apple, but we readily accept gravity’s role in making it fall from the tree. People do terrible things because they feel they have no other choice — to execute them for that is to ensure they never will. Man is an unknowable creature, and few things unsettle it more than forcing it to live within its mystery. “The Life and Deaths of Wilson Shedd” endeavors to deny us any other choice, as its nonlinear timeline nakedly manipulates our feelings about its title character. The more sympathetic he becomes, the more we learn about why he’s been sentenced to lethal injection; the more we learn about the monstrousness of his actions, the more aggressively Nelson’s screenplay — inspired by unspecified true events — insists upon his fundamental humanity. Needless to say, the writer-director does himself a great favor by casting an actor as likable as Scoot McNairy in the part. We first meet Wilson on the day that sets the endgame of his destiny in motion — the day that a beautiful but painfully unloved high school teacher named Karen (a southern-fried Amanda Seyfried) starts her new part-time job teaching fiction to a group of privileged inmates at Oklahoma’s biggest maximum-security prison. Freshly uncoupled from her abusive husband and desperate for the money she needs to pay for her senile dad’s elder care, Karen doesn’t report to the jail any more willingly than her students once did. Most of the inmates react to her presence like chum in the water (there’s only so much a makeup department can do to soften the spectacle of dropping Amanda Seyfried into a room full of horny criminals who probably haven’t seen another woman in months). Wilson, meanwhile, seizes on the opening to play teacher’s pet. A rangy squirrel of a man whose artistic spirit has been as welcome and noticeable throughout his life as a floater in his eye, Wilson is obviously sincere about taking the class seriously. Still, it’s hard to know if that’s because writing sets him free, or because he enjoys having a pretty woman to look at, or because Karen sets his soul on fire in a way that nothing in this world ever has before. It could be all of the above, or it could be none of them — we may never know for sure, and Wilson probably wouldn’t be able to tell us. ~this review continues on IndieWire~

2026-09-13 I Play Rocky (2026) thejoshl primary 2.0 no "Sylvester Stallone... it's like Peter Parker." "Write a freaking script and give yourself the best part!" A pointless endeavor because the genuinely rousing underdog production history behind Rocky already seeped into and was mirrored by that original movie. He put so much of his own unique backstory, personality and self-belief into the sports movie/70s character study formula and elevated it. The real story itself has some undeniably incredible details to it, and some of them here are even realized here with a good sense of humor (shoutout Tracy Letts, primarily, but also a few of the moments that feel like if Stallone were in Boogie Nights ), but the majority of this plays like a series of hollow, soulless Disaster Artist -esque recreations. An exercise in dress-up and impression. At times even a good impression (the lead dude at the right angle is almost indistinguishable from the real deal), but nothing more. Designed in a lab to win the TIFF People's Choice Award on its road toward Oscar, and so shamelessly crowd-pleasing to the point that it will play like the end of Cinema Paradiso for certain age demographic of film critic.
