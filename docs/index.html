2026-09-07 Dekalog (1989) thejoshl primary 5.0 no This review may contain spoilers. Felt good to finally pull the trigger on this and realize that outside of the unifying locale of the austere Warsaw housing block each episode was completely self-contained (shifting not just in terms of narrative focus but also style, structure, etc), and that its reputation as 10 hours of dauntingly drab Polish misery, while not entirely unfounded or untrue, was maybe a bit overstated. How each one of these stories connects to the lofty overarching ten commandments theme that might give this impression is often deliberately ambiguous and oblique, and rather than an intellectual exercise in ideas hovering above and looking down on these characters, they are often gut-wrenchingly and dramatically moral/existential ones driven by a deeply felt intimacy. Even the ones that sound philosophical or analytical in concept and designed to provoke an Ethics 101 class are told with an eerie fable-like sensitivity loaded with human guilt, tragedy and absurdity. V and VI are the obvious two-part stand-out deemed good enough to be expanded into individual features (called A Short Film About Killing/Love ); the former a clinical and ugly jaundiced-filtered In Cold Blood procedural of senseless violence (murder committed by citizen, death penalty by the state), the latter a lonely Rear Window / Blue Velvet peeping tom romance of voyeurism and obsession. But despite this high in the middle, the real power of this is in how it accumulates over the course of 10 haunting hours. By the time I got to VIII and heard the literal ethics professor (who is about to come face-to-face with her own guilt-ridden conscience about her failure to protect a young Jewish girl in the Warsaw ghetto 40 years earlier) start talking about an abortion case study from II, suddenly my mind was drifting back to that shot of wasp trying to escape a cup of strawberries via spoon (as visual metaphor for the soap opera scenario of whether her comatose husband will wake up to find his wife pregnant with another man's child) and I was emotional about it all over again. As a long-form story it's so casual and loose about how these things overlap, and not all of them do. This isn't some hackneyed we're-all-connected drama but a portrait of human communal experience as a series of private worlds all around us we temporarily glimpse the depths of. Maybe the greatest quality of this mini-series though is how it goes about offering no answers or prescriptions to the issues of modern living it raises. When the atheist father in I trusts his glowing computer screens calculation that the outdoor ice his kid wants to skate on is safe because he believes "everything can be counted and measured", it's not a gotcha on him or his lack of religion that the kid dies but a quietly operatic parable on a cold and cruel world that can't fully be understood rationally or logically. And it's a great mood-setter for how it similarly approaches heavy topics of infidelity (IX's suicidal impotency + III's Christmas Eve Before movie walk-and-talk between two cheaters who have tried to move on from their affair) and abuse (VII's double-whammy generational melodrama of an abusive grandmother living vicariously through her statutory raped daughter's child she tries to steal from her). And just when I thought it couldn't get more European, they threw in The Incest One (IV)! It was honestly something of a relief that the last one was a comedy about two bozo brothers and their dead father's stamp collection.

2026-09-06 Possible Love (2026) davidehrlich secondary 4.5 no Another heart-stopping and novelistically layered stunner from modern cinema’s most literary auteur (a fiction author himself), Lee Chang-dong’s “Possible Love” might be dedicated to the memory of Krzysztof Kieślowski’s “Dekalog 10,” but let’s dispel any notion of it being a remake. Conceived independently, and then lumped during pre-production into the same tribute omnibus that kicked off with Asghar Farhadi’s dreadful “Parallel Tales” at Cannes this year this film shares nothing in common with the final episode of Kieślowski’s series beyond the biblical commandment that Lee splays on screen at the very start: “Thou shalt not covet.” And even then, the evidence of that instruction proves elusive in this sprawling drama about two very different couples who are brought together by the violent suppression of a workers’ strike at a major Korean metals factory. Who is coveting what, exactly? It’s simple enough to understand what the film’s characters want on the surface — or at least that’s true of its women. A casually rich dilettante whose pale skin glows with the same lustrousness as her salon-perfect bob, first-time director Ye-ji (Cho Yeo-jeong, similarly cast as the rich mom in “Parasite”) hopes to make a documentary about the declining state of labor across South Korea, with a particular focus on the recent unrest at a business called SP Heavy Industries. Although we see Ye-ji pitching the project to a grant board of some kind, the presumption is that most of her funding — and all of her creative freedom — has been provided by her imperiously chiseled husband Sang-woo (Lee newcomer Zo In-sung), a trust fund type who appears to enjoy a rather frictionless existence. On the other side of the economic divide, the plucky but sad-eyed Mi-ok (played by “Secret Sunshine” actress Jeon Do-yeon, who delivers another once-a-century performance less than 20 years since her first collaboration with Lee) is clearly willing to do whatever it takes to bring her husband back to life. One of the “dead” who lost his job and sense of purpose in the mass layoffs at SP two years ago, her taciturn and implosive Ho-seok (“Peppermint Candy” star Sul Kyung-gu) has been a shell of himself since being injured amid the crackdown that ended the ensuing strike, and Mi-ok hopes that participating in Ye-ji’s doc might allow him to process his trauma — to stop shuffling around like a zombie and snap back into the man she once fell in love with as a teenage seamstress who didn’t have any better options. That he might become a more present father to their young son Cheol, whose current rudderlessness is best expressed by his abject incompetence at Taekwondo. Beyond that simple baseline, the matrix of desire at work in Lee and Oh Jung-mi’s remarkably nuanced script is too slippery to pin down until the movie’s velvet gutpunch of a final scene. Yes, Mi-ok lusts after Ye-ji’s elegant wardrobe and designer handbags, and there’s an unspoken sexual charge in the air whenever she’s alone with Sang-woo (a charge that eventually sets fire to an unforgettable pre-dawn sequence on a private beach in the third act). But it would be unwisely reductive to conclude that the working-class woman is jealous of her hyper-affluent new bestie, just as it would be unhelpfully rash to assume that envy plays a more pivotal role in this story than resentment, or that a film as mottled and probing as “Possible Love” might be content to retrench the divide between Korea’s haves and have-nots. ~this review continues on IndieWire~
