2026-11-12 Ebenezer (2026) davidehrlich secondary 1.5 no A Sauvage and jowly overactor in dire need of career rehabilitation could hardly ask for a better part to play than Ebenezer Scrooge. He’s a cruel miser of a man who comes to learn the error of his ways over the course of a ghost story that preys upon Christian precepts of redemption and forgiveness (along with the more secular warm and fuzzies of seeing a bad guy discover the joys of being good). Indeed, what is “cancel culture” if not a shackled delusion that visits self-made pariahs in the dead of night, and what is Charles Dickens’ “A Christmas Carol” if not a plea to believe that even the most odious of souls are still capable of being saved? It’s been reported that Johnny Depp accepted the title role of Ti West’s “Ebenezer” at a fraction of his usual rate, but I suspect that he probably would have done this movie for free. While the embattled star earned more than $250 million for his work on the “Pirates of the Caribbean” franchise, even that psychotic number pales in comparison to the pricelessness of starring in a major studio Christmas movie that allows him to look just left of the camera and say lines like “I’m not the man I was!” and “I was once good, and I know I can be again!” before his character saves a cherubic little boy from certain death and invites a freezing beggar inside for a warm cup of tea. If an old crank like Scrooge can make his way back toward grace, why not the convicted star of “Mortdecai,” and the alleged perpetrator of several even more serious crimes? Johnny Depp may prefer for that to be framed as a rhetorical question, but his insufferable turn as literature’s most cantankerous moneylender demands an honest answer. For me, that answer is rather straightforward: While Depp has never been the subtlest of actors, his post-Sparrow pivot away from artistic curiosity has distended his talents into such a grotesque caricature of their former charm that “Ebenezer” left me feeling like I may never be able to empathize with his screen presence again. Scrooge is obviously a scenery-chewer’s dream, but the crescent arc he traces from grinch to Christmas miracle is only believable enough to sustain Dickens’ fable if you believe it on both ends. Depp’s performance — an advent calendar of empty affectations without a soul to anchor them, every shot full of face and bereft of feeling — makes that exceedingly difficult. His offscreen unsavoriness certainly doesn’t help, but there’s a more immediate problem when the lights go down, one that makes him a unique case among Hollywood’s tainted array of mega-star actors: He seems to have become very bad at acting. Whatever my feelings about Depp as a human being, the truth is that I didn’t buy him as a glowering monster any more than I bought him as a beaming man who’s grown “better than his word.” An unengaging Ebenezer is a tough break for a movie called “Ebenezer,” and screenwriter Nathaniel Halpern’s fidelity to the source material doesn’t exactly leave West with a wealth of opportunities to compensate for such fatal miscasting. West’s take on the holiday classic is faithful to the core — no Muppets, no ghosts of girlfriends past, no uncanny mo-capped Jim Carrey. As you might expect from the director of indie genre standouts like “Pearl” and “The House of the Devil,” West’s big idea is to scare the Dickens out of us by turning up the volume on this story’s implicit scariness. Sometimes literally, as this PG-13 carol offers a small handful of gentle jump-scares. ~this review continues on IndieWire~

2026-11-11 How to Rob a Bank (2026) davidehrlich secondary 2.5 no The major Hollywood studios tend to shy away from the subject of class warfare because they know they can’t pull them off with a straight face. But young people in this country are increasingly animated by radical leftist ideas like “earning a livable wage” and “being able to buy a home before they die,” and at a certain point the movies will have to meet them where they live. Perhaps that point is now. How else to explain why Jeff Bezos would fund a Robin Hood-esque heist thriller that not only encourages its target demographic to commit armed robberies, but also kinda sorta teaches them how? (No one is going to confuse a David Leitch movie for “The Anarchist Cookbook.”) At a time when internet activists like Hasan Piker have come to occupy more space in the public imagination than Marvel superheroes, selling the masses on fanciful stories about the downward redistribution of wealth might be the billionaire class’ best protection against the threat of it actually happening in real life. It’s a lot more fun than paying a proportional tax rate, at least. Of course, the poppy and frenetic “How to Rob a Bank” wouldn’t dare to take aim at massive corporations that widen the wealth gap by destroying small businesses, normalizing exploitative labor practices, and accelerating worldwide environmental collapse. This is very specifically a film about how banks are bad and should be fucked with, an opinion that the founder of Amazon might actually share with the average ticket-buyer. And it’s not like the director of “Bullet Train” suddenly made a serious call to action that’s poised to inspire a wave of copycat robberies. Shot with all the propulsiveness of “Trainspotting” but almost none of its unbridled lust for life, Leitch’s YouTube-era fairy tale is no more dangerous to America’s power structures than “Verity” or “Project Hail Mary” (to name some of its studio’s other recent titles). Compared to the lawlessness of “Grand Theft Auto VI,” it might as well be “Nintendo Switch Sports Resort.” Still, its cast is hot, its heart is in the right place, and while the Amazon Punk of it all has a tendency to make its fantasy seem more like a sale, there’s something indivisibly real and relatable to the premise of a heist movie whose characters feel like they have to steal the most basic principles of the life they were promised. Ryan (Nicolas Hoult, channeling a very effective smidgeon of the destructive intensity that made him so terrifying in “The Order”) isn’t trying to get rich or spark a revolution, he and his accomplices just want a fair shot at the same thing that previous generations have been afforded as part of the American contract: The chance to support his family, and maybe a fair shot at revenge against the people and/or system who denied him that opportunity. In most respects, that shot has never been more unfair than it is now, but the internet — which has become the most powerful system for socioeconomic oppression this side of sharecropping — has turned attention into a currency more valuable than cash, and young people are still inventing new ways to harness those riches for themselves. For Ryan, that process starts with stealing money the old-fashioned way, as he and his four-person crew film themselves knocking off every bank in Pittsburgh (their identities protected by giant animal masks). But the cash seems to be less important than the content that it buys them, as the gang’s real passion is reserved for the splashy, hi-octane YouTube tutorials that Hugo (an upbeat Rhenzy Feliz) cuts together and posts online after each job. “You know what’s more powerful than a gun?,” Ryan says. “An audience.” That’s us! The magnetic pull these two have is both scary and liberating because unlike the wealthy people around them both are aware of how frank and brutal one needs to be to socially/financially climb in this rotten world. Unfortunately, as much as I see why this was shocking and salacious for the time, I think Robert Wise plays it all a little too tame after the opening Jim Thompson kitchen double-murder, which is so sudden and casual for these characters that I expected it to turn into more a perverse crime spree story whereas instead it mostly settles into a mansion melodrama. I didn't realize at the time those cartoons were teaching me the language of the great silent era comedians, so it wasn't until I started watching Keaton, Chaplin, Lloyd, etc (and really thinking about them), that it occurred to me what their artistry really was. That what I'd really been laughing at all this time was in some ways real existential pain and suffering, and that through that laughter I was also bonding and feeling for these clowns as profoundly as I might a really well-done melodrama. So it was a really beautiful surprise that that cathartic realization I had later in life was quite literally the subject of this movie. What if Sisyphus and the boulder were two lonely souls with a mutually-agreed-upon, symbiotic arrangement, and ultimately kind of loved each other? That's a purity we can't let any corporation poison or exploit.
