2026-08-21 The End of Oak Street (2026) thejoshl primary 4.0 no This review may contain spoilers. Always impressive to me when a filmmaker's "one for them", get-out-of-(director's)-jail-free-card movie is still distinctly theirs. Similarly to how It Follows was a best-case scenario in terms of some dude from the suburbs of Michigan seemingly coming out of nowhere and dropping a low-budget Carpenter pastiche, this is about the same for an indie filmmaker graduating to a studio budget and doing their Spielberg/Amblin cover. Helps that it also has an amusingly goofy Twilight Zone premise that involves a portal suddenly transporting an 80s suburb straight out of ET or Poltergeist into the Jurassic era, and gleefully turning a few square blocks into its own Lost World monster movie park to gruesomely play around in. A dinosaur storming its way across various front lawns and backyards is a strikingly silly image that I will confess to being a little worried would lean too far into childhood nostalgia for DRM who I somehow never realized was an 80s kid until this movie. Thankfully, this movie was much weirder and nastier than expected, and really pushes its premise to places that had me thinking of qualities I find myself drawn to in M. Night Shyamalan and Richard Kelly more than even Spielberg—even if that dung heap is unmistakable. Firstly, despite all the elegant setpiece machinery (Gioulakis once again doing some spectacular craftmanship with all the slow pans/zooms/tracking shots and gorgeous widescreen, negative space heavy framing), there's a legit mean streak to the monster mayhem. When all the effectively controlled suspense-building patiently using reflections or backgrounds or obscured blocking (straight from the Spielberg economy playbook) gives way to the stomping and chomping it's with a level of casually harrowing ruthlessness that's almost comical. The amount of blood splatter, sudden senseless collateral damage, and sheer body count is pushing PG-13 about as hard as anything since Temple of Doom . Every muscular neighborhood chase (with fantastic visual use of fences, driveways, etc) featuring corpses being feasted on in various corners of the frame, and every household weapon about as useless against the animalistic home invaders as they should be. Kid sitting beside me had to cover his eyes through a lot of it. Secondly, and maybe more importantly, despite some of the humor and aw-shucks suburban archetype family character writing, there's a notable sense of melancholy to its fantasy. Taking the time to linger on the kids confusion and sadness (“is this the end of the world?”), on imagery that reckons with apocalyptic climate anxiety, and on the horrifically personal loss of a parent (jaw quite literally dropped at the T. rex bit) that requires some Donnie Darko wormhole time travel trickery to undo. I get the mixed response to the ending which on its surface reads as a tidy wrap-up after it had just violently raised the bar as to what it was willing to viciously do to its characters, but I felt there was a certain level of knowingly absurd wish-fulfillment to how it goes about restoring its 80s Americana domesticity after quite literally exposing its fragility, tearing its limbs off, and detonating it to the point of near-extinction for most of its runtime. “We’re like butterflies who flutter for a day and think it’s forever." MVPs: Anne Hathaway, the dog, the giant snake who goes 🙂
