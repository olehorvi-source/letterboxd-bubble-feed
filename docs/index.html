2026-11-05 Hen (2025) davidehrlich secondary 3.0 no Among the bleakest films ever made, Werner Herzog’s “Stroszek” (1977) famously ends not with the suicide of its title character, but with a haunting coda in which a chicken pecks at a miniature piano inside a surreal roadside tourist trap’s coin-operated display. It’s a visual metaphor for the inescapable banality of life on Earth, and for the dehumanizing work people are forced to do to disguise the many failures of the American Dream. It’s also the last thing that Joy Division frontman Ian Curtis saw before he killed himself. “Look into the eyes of a chicken,” Herzog has said, “and you will see real stupidity. It is a kind of bottomless stupidity, a fiending stupidity. They are the most horrifying, cannibalistic, and nightmarish creatures in the world.” That sentiment has long endured as modern cinema’s definitive stance on chickens (despite Aardman’s best efforts), but now, after witnessing the 50 years of entropy that followed “Stroszek,” Hungarian filmmaker György Pálfi is crying fowl. Chickens are not the most horrifying, cannibalistic, and nightmarish creatures in the world, he contends. We are. How can you tell? Easy: Just look at us through the eyes of a chicken. A cute, cockeyed yarn that unspools like a feathered cross between “EO” and “Flow,” Pálfi’s “Hen” tells the story of a jet-black Leghorn chicken who escapes a Greek factory farm after she’s plucked from the rest of her flock because of the color of her plumage. A worker decides to take the bird home so that his wife can serve it for dinner, but our sly heroine — played by a brave hen named Feri, in addition to her seven indistinguishable stunt doubles — makes a daring escape when her captor stops his truck to refuel. But she’s snapped up again by the teeth of a dog named Titan, who carries the chicken back to the ramshackle seaside restaurant where his owner (Yannis Kokiasmenos) has much bigger problems than what to serve for dinner. If the precise contours of those problems are a bit difficult to deduce, that’s probably because — as we’ve already established — this movie is told through the eyes of a chicken. Sometimes literally, thanks to Pálfi’s judicious deployment of POV, which is useful for times when our ruffled protagonist is, say, trapped inside a cardboard box (there’s an excellent bit where the hen starts moving the cardboard box from the inside, a “Metal Gear Solid”-inspired visual gag that proves typical of the director’s wry humor). And what does a chicken know of Europe’s ongoing migrant crisis? Of a protest between angry citizens and riot police? Of human trafficking, and the complications thereof? To judge by the film’s regular assortment of amusingly blank reaction shots, I’d wager “very little.” The titular hen doesn’t even think to look both ways when she crosses the road after flying the coop, let alone have a good answer as to why she’s doing it. ~this review continues on IndieWire~

I need to know someone like I know myself.” “You think you know me like that?” It’s hard to watch this extremely confident 90s neo-noir by the Wachowski sisters and not read into the closeted nature of not just its literal lesbian romance but its formal genre playfulness too. Which sees the debut filmmakers take the conventional trapped box structure of a femme fatale and crime-gone-wrong gangster-thriller, milk it for as much claustrophobic tension and kinetic style as one possibly could, and then subversively push against the prudish, punishing, often tragic ends of these stories to locate legitimate liberation and an earnest chance at a new life/identity. In many ways it’s sort of like if Blood Simple had a happy ending, but instead of that feeling like an easy cop out for Violet and Corky (Gina Gershon and Jennifer Tilly, both electric), it feels like a dangerous but achievable fantasy/dream. Maybe the most impressive aspect of this is not just how filled with erotic yearning it is (that shot of Gershon twisting the wet sink pipe in the foreground with Tilly’s legs/hips in her black dress in the background of the frame behind her… the overhead shot of them on the phone on opposite sides of the apartments wall reaching out for each other…) but how good of a straight up action-thriller it is on such a modest budget too. Bill Pope shoots the absolute shit out of all the overhead and tracking maneuvers through the cramped space as it goes through all its effective logistical schemes and twists, and Joey Pants gives one of the best hateable frantic slimeball performances you’ll ever see as Violet’s dangerous mobster money laundering boyfriend Caesar. The stretch in the film after Corky has laid out their heist plan (in a really well-done voiceover montage) and Tilly must both seductively perform her role within in it like a Barbara Stanwyck in Double Indemnity /Kathleen Turner in Body Heat and improvise her way through Caesar’s unpredictable and volatile response to it is exactly what you want out a movie like this. Unbearably tense thriller scenarios motivated by flawed (occasionally resourceful!) character decision-making that results in torture, cover-ups, cat-and-mouse chases, and grisly slow-motion shootouts. That image of the blood flowing into the white paint… Just the best. It’s such an impressive debut you can see exactly why they were given the money for The Matrix , and as much as I love the expensive experimental digital maximalism of Speed Racer and earnestly vast ambition of Cloud Atlas , part of me wishes the Wachowskis would consider scaling themselves back down to something like this again. Also, kind of fun to think about in the context of Hitchock's Rope , in terms of establishing a cinematic history of thrillers about being gay and doing crimes (and tying people up).
