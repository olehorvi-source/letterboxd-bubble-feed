2026-09-13 The Face of Horror (2026) thejoshl primary 4.0 no Anna Biller continues her project of playfully putting feminist gender war theory up against some of the most lovingly hand-crafted 60s analog fetish filmmaking pastiche you will ever witness. This time moving away from the realm of sexploitation and into pure gothic Hammer horror, with some Roger Corman (especially those Edgar Allen Poe adaptations) and Mario Bava thrown in for good measure. This one based on an old Kabuki female revenge/ghost story, repurposed to take place in medieval England and centered around a beautiful farmer's daughter whose performatively chivalrous knight husband is actually a misogynistic serial killing scoundrel. His monstrous military violence and total disregard of women barely disguised by his prince charming façade, and eventually resulting in a scheme to rid of himself of his wife (not before horribly disfiguring her!) and dooming himself to cathartically hellish punishment. Similarly to The Love Witch , this kind of requires you to be a sicko that enjoys even the shaggy, stilted flaws of this era, but if you can get on its wavelength there's real poetry and humor to how she goes about turning her obsession with all this over-stylized retro "beautiful gowns" lavishness and incorporates it into her mission to vengefully reclaim this iconography from an era where sexism was in more intensely plain sight. And thus could be dealt with in similarly broad genre strokes! It goes without saying that the deep focus 35mm lensing, colorful lighting, meticulous sets/costumes, red paint gore, and old-school dreaminess is drop-dead gorgeous, and at times probably luxuriated in for the sake of it. But this fixation on beautifully artificial, uncannily out-of-time surfaces also serves as a comically pointed contradiction to the rotten underlying medieval melodrama dynamic. Biller and her actors gleefully leaning into the campy over-acting, the ridiculous retrograde battle of the sexes dialogue, the systemic critique of how blatant and encouraged men's bad behavior is, and taking the central idea that a woman's value in this world stems the whether or not she can maintain her performative, objectifying beauty, and taking it to violently upsetting extremes. Rotting/charred corpses, decapitated heads, and protruding eyeballs galore, a Masque of Red Death -esque musical sequence about how death makes us all equal (rich-poor, men-women), a series of hallucinatory Black Sabbath Italian horror ghost-nightmare setpieces. Whether it works for you or not, no one else is making movies like this.

2026-09-13 Misty Green (2026) davidehrlich secondary 2.5 no A raw and biting — or at least vigorously gumming — showbiz character study about a faded Black actress whose entire adult life has been shaped by the peaks and valleys of living in a place that often seems flat on the surface, Chris Rock’s “Misty Green” is preoccupied with the relationship between feast and famine from the moment it starts, and by the Los Angeles-specific tendency for those two conditions to remain worlds apart even when they overlap. Even when they walk right past each other on Hollywood Blvd., or audition for the same role on a studio lot. Fortune is a fickle thing: One day you’re winning some award on TV, and the next day, a short 18 years later, you’re being evicted from your dead sugar daddy’s beachside pied-à-tierre because he left it to his wife in his will. That’s la la land for ya. As Rock takes pains to remind us in the photo montage that opens his latest and most serrated film (a film that shares its setting’s lack of subtlety), L.A. is a city where world-class museums are just down the block from Skid Row, where the Oscars are hosted inside of a hideous shopping mall, and where the paparazzi thoughtlessly climb over homeless people to snap photos of starlets like Lindsay Lohan (to name the first of several celebrities this movie catches with a random stray). How is anyone supposed to stop chasing the bag when it feels like, even at their lowest ebb, they’re still always close enough to reach out and grab it? By the same token, how is anyone supposed to have the bag when there’s almost no degree of success that can ensure they’ll let you keep it? If such a degree of success even exists, it’s certainly not available to a woman like Misty. Played to wearily ferocious perfection by “Slow Horses” powerhouse Rosalind Eleazar in what should be a star-making performance (but you never know in this town!), the almost 40-year-old Misty has developed a full script of excuses for the downward spiral she’s been on since winning an Emmy for a TV show called “Red Eye” in 2008. “Too Black to be with Leo, not Black enough to be with Damson, not a nepo baby or a Brit.” She lays it out with a battle-worn intensity that deflects attention from what she’s trying hard not to say; we think “it’s a funny meta-joke that Eleazar is a Brit,” and “is Damson Idris really famous enough to belong in that sentence?,” and not “what has Misty done for the last two decades besides making excuses for herself and getting cozy with some old guy who let her live in his spare apartment?” We get a partial answer to that question after she bumps into Jordan, the director whose “Cinderella” remake was going to launch Misty into the stratosphere until she got coked up out of control and punched him in the face on set (Rock plays the part himself, wise and resigned in a role that warmly reflects how the onetime firebrand has softened with age). She’s so deeply internalized Hollywood’s treatment of Black women that she can’t even give herself a break. So yeah, it’s true that Misty is a complicated person in a business that’s still painfully simple in its approach to race, age, and second chances, but it’s also true that her inestimable talents as an actress are equaled by her gift for self-sabotage. It’s never just one or the other in her adopted city — it’s always both right on top of each other. ~this review continues on IndieWire~
