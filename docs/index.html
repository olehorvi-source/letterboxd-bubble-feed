2026-11-27 The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth (2026) thejoshl primary 3.0 no Tarantino goes back to Elmore Leonard for the first time since Jackie Brown and drops his easygoing stuntman turned Hollywood drifter-fixer Cliff Booth into the sort of industry gangster neo-noir Chili Palmer might have found himself in. Pitt slides right back into the natural confident charm that made Cliff so enjoyable to hangout with in Once Upon A Time In Hollywood , as he went about his nostalgic analog routines like a childish California daydream. One that was then complicated by both the questionable details of violent, outmoded men like this (a war veteran/wife killer capable of disturbingly impulsive and sadistic physical force) and the melancholy realization of the limitations of this male-bonding, history-rewriting power fantasy that will never exist outside the artificial glow of the movie screen. In some ways, Cliff is sort of the less-evil twin to Death Proof ’s Stuntman Mike, one of the most overt autocritique characters in the man’s catalog: a voyeur-slasher misogynist serial killer from the analog era that uses his car as a tool to harm women and is subsequently castrated by Uma Thurman’s stunt double only a few years after the horrific car crash accident on the set of Kill Bill . Making it all the more impressive that despite this baggage (and that of the two men who have brought this character to life), Cliff is just such a relaxed hang. Despite its upsetting introduction early on, for about 90 minutes or so I legitimately forgot there even was a violent hitman plot climax we were headed towards... Similarly to the original film, it’s a feat alone that I could’ve easily spent another hour in the projector booth, or drinking canned beer and watching old movies on TV with Peter Weller, or doing sexy/witty (sadomasochistic?!) rom-com banter with Elizabeth Debicki around all the various tennis courts, racetracks, bars, etc (I was quite literally every periphery character who remarked on how tall she is); honestly, even Scott Caan’s Tarantino impression is quite fun. Ultimately, I can’t say I had anything less than a good time with this. To address the elephant in the room though, I don’t really understand the Fincher of it all tbh. I mean, I understand how this literally came about, with Tarantino being so bizarrely nitpicky about what his tenth and “final” movie is going to be, and Fincher being a lifelong collaborator of Pitt’s that he obviously recruited to take over a script he liked. Part of me was even a little excited about how his polar-opposite filmmaking fetish/philosophy (obsessively razor-sharp digital fluidity) would interact with such shaggy, analog material. The answer: not all that cleanly or elegantly imo. Maybe some will find a way to argue the messiness and uncanniness is interesting or productively anachronistic, but for me, as much as I think Fincher is an undeniable master of his specific toolkit, I spent a lot of time wondering if his skills were actually providing any sort of additive here. Certainly, there are some setpieces, character moments, even a few signature Fincher inserts/edits that work, but for every one of them there was just something about it that kinda gnawed at me too. Maybe it’s the dark, muted look he opted for, maybe it’s all the smooth, overly manicured CG (and, sigh, A.I.) touch-ups he likes to do. But even shots as simple as a character driving around Hollywood or recreations of fake in-universe movies/TV (pleasurably tactile highlights in the last film!), just kinda look like eyesores here. And that’s projected in laser IMAX, not on a compressed streamer where most will see this! Fincher’s deliberately clinical, off-putting, anti-cathartic handling of violence (and how he creates the illusion of manic rapid-fire chaos through intensely fussy shooting/staging of action) is one of the few things I think he approaches so differently than Tarantino it has an interesting effect on the overall film I guess. Especially its weird, sudden, darker-than-expected finale which in classic weary, 70s revisionist western style has a bit of fun in a bar before acknowledging that the rooms where these violent legends are born from (and that the industry likes to gossip about) are probably not all that pleasant to actually be in when shit hits the fan. Open to this one growing on me like the last one did but on first pass it definitely feels like some fundamental (movie) magic was lost here amongst a scattering of good individual pieces. Or maybe we just have a case of two sodas that shouldn’t be poured into the same New Beverly cup.

2026-11-26 Fjord (2026) thejoshl primary 2.0 no Mungiu is a skilled naturalist when it comes to shooting, staging and creating a social realist environment for his actors—his passion is very clearly in how to methodically achieve a tonal ambiguity that serves the political systems his characters are trapped in. Which makes the caricatured debate class bullshit of this one so frustrating. It's generally handsomely made and well-acted (at least during the non-mouthpiece scenes), but ultimately so unconvincing and difficult not to see through the mechanical way it approaches its "provocative" subject matter. Taking a highly publicized child-abuse case and going out of its way to present an extremely generous view of a conservative, religious fundamentalist immigrant family, who only occasionally slaps their children. Meanwhile having them face off against the most Kafkaesque of a progressive liberal atheist bureaucracy that is motivated by an intolerance of their traditional (moral) values and weaponizes scary state power in order to make an example of and ostracize them. I'm of the personal belief that there are certainly interesting, uncomfortable conversations to be had when it comes politically enforcing a progressive multicultural society, and dealing with the fact that a lot of fundamentally good people deserving of support and sympathy can also have regressive views on certain subjects (especially when its wrapped up in familial/communal indoctrination), but I don't think many of them are actually being had in this film tbh. It certainly makes broad gestures towards them, but in a condescending and self-satisfied manner that feels closer to a bloated, ponderous Euroart version of that South Park episode where the Mormons move in next door.
