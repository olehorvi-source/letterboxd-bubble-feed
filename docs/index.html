2026-08-11 Pecker (1998) brofromanother primary no During the Make the Case pre-show last night at the Paradise (thanks to Simon Ennis for selecting the movie) the programmers used a quote from Manohla Dargis to stand in for the widespread critical disparagement of Pecker: "John Waters," I'm paraphrasing here but basically this is verbatim, "still doesn't know how to make a movie." Dargis of course knows how to watch a movie: she's a really good critic and not the type to miss things that have been intentionally put on screen for her consideration. But the idea of Pecker as 1) being badly crafted 2) worse for that fact belies the point of the exercise. “If my pictures were better,” says Edward Furlong’s “Teenage Weegee” to the culture vulture apprising his wares, “they wouldn’t be as great;” Waters has said Pecker is not his surrogate here (that’d be the little girl addicted to sugary snacks and Cola to the point of needing to be medicated) but darned if he doesn’t seem to be ventriloquizing his art through him – such as it is. Pecker is not artful but it has something to say about art. A few things in fact: that it’s a study of beauty; that it’s rooted in love; that it’s easily commodified but also – Waters being a true optimist – also potentially rescued from commodification. It also says that the commodifiers themselves are not bad people, just a little out to lunch, and themselves worthy and valuable subjects of affectionate deconstruction; this is a movie where Cindy Sherman not only shows up but shows she’s in on the joke (she offers the little sugar addict a Valium). The bit where a homeless couple crashes an invite only dinner nods, however obloquely, towards Viridiana, and Waters – who’s spoken of Bunuel reverently in the same breath as Bergman and Hershell Gordon Lewis – had aged by 1998 into a similar, discreetly charming style of bourgeois critique. Not mean, not lacerating, not defiant, but also not necessarily wimpy either; the jokes here include a cluster of Baltimore-ian seniors chanting angrily to “bring back Bush!” (double entendre intended) and Pecker and his laundomat-Mussolini sweetie Shelley (Christina Ricci) reconciling by hooking up loudly in a voting booth – privacy in public; sex as civic duty. Now as to whether Waters knows how to make a movie: of course he does. He makes it his way, which means quickly, cheaply, but still with a sense of pacing, timing, and – crucial for a character-driven independent comedy – a knack for getting the most out of his actors, each according to their ability. Furlong is not as good an actor as, say, Tobey Maguire, who was originally offered the role, and possibly would have brought more depth and shading and technique. But his smiley blankness – he’s like a benign cipher – works for a kid who’s curious about everyone and everything except himself, and who in the end really wants to do right by his family and friends and his talent, which the movie believes is real (a sour, obvious version of Pecker would imply that his work was fraudulent; even the satirical parodies of art-world prose hyping his "humane Diane Arbus" portraits err on the side of credibility). Writing is part of filmmaking and Waters is smart enough to not contrive any truly heavy-handed melodrama as his hero’s celebrity ascends and implodes; it’s all light and sweet and we know it’ll be alright. Ricci, who’s a great actor, is funnier and cuter and more distinctive in Pecker than Buffalo 66 or Opposite of Sex or what have you; I love how zealously she guards her laundromat’s code of conduct, and how she kisses the ground when their charter bus returns from New York to Baltimore (here *she’s* playing John Waters, if only for a moment). I’d like to bring up one other review - not cited during the pre-show – which was Roger Ebert’s two-star pan. Pretty rich for a guy who moralized about Pink Flamingos back in 1975 to bemoan the tension between Waters’ anarchic past and his mellowed-out maturity; to complain that nothing shocking happens. But he’s just so wrong about the subplot involving the Virgin Mary statue, which he says “could have provided comic possibilities, but doesn’t.” It sure does provide comic possibilities – a Catholic granny who claims her weird puppet speaks for God but keeps moving her lips during the benedictions; it’s funny! – but it also provides another quote that, like, the one about not being good being a source of greatness, proposes a way of watching the film as a whole. “Full of grace, full of grace,” she intones, over and over. “Full of grace.” She’s correct. “Better if Mary had been an art critic,” snarked Ebert. Wrong. She should have been a film critic.
