2026-09-05 45 Years (2015) thejoshl primary 4.0 yes This review may contain spoilers. Honestly still as devastated by this as much as I was a decade ago. Haigh walks such a fine line between writing this relationship between Rampling and Courtney's elderly couple Kate & George to have an understated naturalism to it (building a sense of lived-in, low-stakes routine that comes with decades of living together), but then also deploying expertly controlled framing and blocking around their house/town that intimately allow us into Kate's feeling that something has disrupted this space. Shot choices that feel casually loaded by this unspoken, recontextualizing history that George already knows and the and Kate comes to painfully realize with an unbearable patience. Knowing the destination ahead of time on this watch I had a lump in my throat right from the opening scene, when you can already sense George is shell-shocked by the information that the body of his previous lover who fell during during a hike through the Swiss mountains (and who he intended to marry before Kate) has been recovered, and in the week leading up to his 45th anniversary party becomes incapable of hiding his devastation/obsession from Kate. Subtly shifting his behavior and leaking out details about his past that reveal their entire life together as a form of fiction she was unaware of. Everything from her hair, to their decision to not have kids, to their lack of photos on the wall, to the songs they dance to... all of it was in some way or another informed by (or a ghostly reflection of) this other person she never knew. Despite the quiet British melodrama register this ultimately plays out in, emotionally/thematically it's as harrowing as if Scottie in Vertigo married and grew old with his fantasy recreation and slowly came to realize it was unsustainable without telling his other half. A form of heartache and disappointment you're used to seeing from the perspective of a male protagonist, and here deliberately told from the tragic angle of his wife suddenly forced to reckon with the fact that the love of her life (consciously, or unconsciously) has been a dishonest coward performing a role he settled for. A macro picture she’s only able to see in plain view when it's already too late to do anything about it, and he's becomes too heartbroken and lazy to continue to sincerely do the micro work anymore. "You can't just back out of things simply because you don't feel like it... even when you have reasons." The look on Rampling's face when she's in the attic flipping through the carousal slide projector and when she appears to really listen to the lyrics of "Smoke Gets In Your Eyes" for the first time. Just an unreal performance.

2026-09-05 A Long Winter (2026) davidehrlich secondary 3.0 no Andrew Haigh is a master of restraint, a talent that he’s often weaponized against his own creations to beautiful and shattering effect. “All of Us Strangers,” his most powerful feature so far, achieved its singularly cosmic force by crushing Andrew Scott’s grief-stricken gay everyman under the weight of his own shame, the sharp but glancing angle of Haigh’s direction gradually freighting the character’s regrets with the gravity of a black hole. Adapted from the Colm Tóibín novella of the same name (the rights to which Haigh reserved almost two decades ago, well before “Weekend” had first suggested that he might be a worthy steward of a famous author’s work), “A Long Winter” finds the English filmmaker a long way from home, but still on some very familiar terrain — and I don’t just mean the rugged American West, where he previously shot 2017’s “Lean on Pete.” Turning his attention to the fringes of a remote Colorado ghost town that seems adrift in a cold gray limbo between the heavens above and the world below, Haigh returns with another immaculately crafted and agonizingly repressed queer drama about the pains of self-preservation, the strength required to stand on one’s own two feet some day, and — most of all — the question of how people cope with their quiet desperation without compromising themselves to death along the way. Only this time, the director’s signature restraint does more to suffocate his protagonist than it does to set him free. Much like its closeted young hero, the first-born son of a brittle family that’s been stuck in survival mode ever since the local mine shut down, the 1951-set “A Long Winter” is both sturdy as oak and withdrawn to the point of implosion. But Mike, in stark contrast to the even more diffident leads I’ve come to expect from Haigh, quickly makes clear that he refuses to be as reined in as the film around him (which in turn makes the film around him feel all the more constrictive). Played by a resilient and clear-eyed Fred Hechinger, the enduring warmth of his performance a small miracle in a movie frigid enough to make “The Turin Horse” feel like a tropical breeze by comparison, Mike rejects the implication that he should be defined by his setting, or by how Haigh chooses to frame it. Here, his lead character isn’t dimensionalized by Haigh’s reserve so much as he’s at risk of being diminished by it. Easy as it is to root for Mike to slip out from his father’s petrified shadow and make his way to “the other side of the mountain,” as it were, Haigh’s film — despite brimming with exquisite detail and finely rendered feeling — is too snowed in to support him on that journey. ~this review continues on IndieWire~

2026-09-05 Tender Loving Care (2026) davidehrlich secondary 4.0 no Said to be Mike Leigh’s final film, the simple but profoundly affecting “Tender Loving Care” confronts the dominant theme of his career — the practice and value of communal support in the face of an unforgiving world — with the directness of a closing statement. Here, in an exquisitely rendered micro-portrait about a week in the lives of two sparkling London social workers, Leigh gets right to the heart of the matter. The result is one of his most formidable stories in spite of its scope; spartan and straightforward, absent plot complication but awash in pointillistic detail, this so-called swan song collects its attention into a bracingly humane look at conflicting networks of care. It exalts in what love and tenderness can do for a person, and just as honestly pays heed to what they can’t. Rosie (played by Leigh favorite Alice Bailey Johnson) has made a certain kind of peace with those limitations, even if that peace is more tenuous than she might care to admit. A battle-tested social worker whose name reflects her cheerful bedside manner, she practically beams through the sober first scene of the film — a hospital check-in on an unhoused and non-verbal drifter who’s just suffered a mild heart attack. Rosie clocks the man’s vacant stare, and knows full well that the social safety net isn’t at all strong enough to hold his weight, but she offers him every bit of the compassion he deserves. Sometimes, that’s all the government will allow her to give. It’s not enough, but it isn’t nothing either. Not the way Rosie doles it out. In return for her compassion, Rosie is (self) rewarded with a huge glass of wine in her flat at the end of the day. She doesn’t really like the fireman she’s been seeing (the big third date is scheduled for the upcoming weekend), but at least that’s something to look forward to. More promising for Rosie, at least in the short term, is that her greatest source of strength and comfort — her life-long BFF Amy, 40 years old with so much love to give — has moved back in with her after being dumped by her dodgy boyfriend of the last half-decade. Luminously possessed by “Widow’s Bay” star Kate O’Flynn, whose sweetly pained smile of a performance evokes a less pathological take on the joy that Sally Hawkins brought to “Happy-Go-Lucky,” Amy is so upbeat that she almost makes Rosie seem like Eeyore by comparison. They drink in their sweatpants, they dance around the apartment, they call each other sexy bitches. Most of all, they make it viscerally clear that they are and always have been there for each other; no one, save perhaps Mike Leigh himself, better understands how far that kind of backing can go, and how devastating it can be to live without it. ~this review continues on IndieWire~
